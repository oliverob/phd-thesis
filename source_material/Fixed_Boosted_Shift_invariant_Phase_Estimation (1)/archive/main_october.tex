\section{Introduction}
Sampling thermal (Gibbs) states of physical systems is one of the most credible applications for future fault-tolerant quantum computers. Thermal expectation values are of interest across quantum chemistry, condensed matter physics and high energy physics, and classically they are almost all computed by Markov chain Monte Carlo. For many fermionic or frustrated spin systems this approach runs into the sign problem as the weights are not all positive. As the average sign decays exponentially with system size and inverse temperature, the number of samples necessary to resolve the observable grows exponentially \cite{Troyer_2005}. A quantum computer can directly interact with the state rather than only with a signed decomposition, so it does not encounter the sign problem. The classical computer is attempting to replicate the interference that physics gives for free on a quantum computer.

The original quantum algorithm invented for performing this sampling was the Quantum Metropolis algorithm of Temme et. al. \cite{Temme_2011}. This was a direct quantum analogue of Metropolis-Hastings: propose a random unitary move then accept or reject the move based upon the energy before and after the move. The principal challenge of creating a quantum analogue of the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm was rejecting a move. In the classical setting one can either copy the state before the move or simply undo the move after rejecting, however, in the quantum setting copying the initial state is forbidden by the no-cloning principle and simply reversing the proposed unitary does not undo the disturbance caused by the accept/reject measurement. Temme et. al. \cite{Temme_2011} navigated around these challenges by using a Marriott-Watrous \cite{MarriottWatrous} inspired rewinding procedure to approximately return the system to its previous energy upon rejecting a move.

The rewinding procedure requires a modified quantum phase estimation (QPE) protocol, which coherently encodes an estimate of the system’s energy in an ancilla register. This register is reused without resetting it during rewinding, hence the protocol must work even when the register is not initialised to $\ket{0}$. The original construction therefore required QPE to be ``shift-invariant": starting from a register value $p$ rather than zero simply translates the output by $p$. It also required ``boosting" to suppress the tails of the energy-estimation amplitudes, reducing the weight of estimates far from the true energy. Thus, the original Quantum Metropolis algorithm depended upon a  ``boosted shift-invariant" quantum phase estimation protocol that applied a shift to an ancilla register within a given bound of the true energy (with $\tilde E_j = \frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_j$ for an $\eta$ ancilla energy register):
\begin{multline}
  \ket{\psi_j}\ket{p} \;\mapsto\; \sum_{s} \alpha(E_j,s)\,\ket{\psi_j}\ket{p+s},
  \\\alpha(E_j,s) = 0 \ \text{ for } |s-\tilde E_j|_{2^{\eta}} > \mu .
  \label{eq:Boostedshiftinvariantqpe}
\end{multline}
where $\ket{\psi_j}$ is an energy eigenstates with energy $E_j$, $\mu$ is the allowed error in register units, and $|\cdot|_{2^\eta}$ measures distance accounting for the register’s wraparound.

Recently the field has been reshaped by a new paradigm based on Davies-generator Lindbladian evolution \cite{chen2023quantumthermalstatepreparation,Kastoryano2014QuantumGS,shtanko2021preparing,chen2021fast,chen2023efficient,rall2023thermal,gilyen2024quantum,ding2025efficient,ding2025end,guo2025designing,chen2025efficient, hahn2026efficient, scandi2026thermalization,leng2026accelerating,rouze2026optimal,lloyd2026quantum,Rouz__2026} which has largely displaced the Metropolis approach in the literature. This current dominance of Lindbaldian samplers is in no small part due to the no-go theorem contained in Appendix H of \cite{chen2023quantumthermalstatepreparation}. This proved it is impossible to implement the ``boosted shift-invariant" quantum phase estimation protocol \eqref{eq:Boostedshiftinvariantqpe} that the original Quantum Metropolis algorithm required.  The original implementation \cite{Temme_2011} suggested taking the median of multiple QPE runs which fails to be ``shift-invariant" as the difference of medians is not necessarily close to the median of the differences. This no-go theorem is widely regarded as closing the book on Quantum Metropolis sampling. However, one recent work \cite{jiang2024quantummetropolissamplingweak} partially revived it by avoiding the rewinding protocol and hence the need for this impossible protocol via a weak measurement protocol at the cost of a slowdown factor that directly hampers the rate of convergence.

A different route to discrete-time Metropolis-type dynamics is taken in \cite{gilyen2024quantum}, which constructs efficiently implementable discrete-time channels that are exactly detailed balanced, and shows how Metropolis-style samplers that estimate energies before and after a jump can be made exactly detailed balanced. There the rejection step is a single additional Kraus operator implemented by quantum singular value transformation, so no Marriott--Watrous rewinding, and hence no shift-invariant phase estimation, is required; they also prove a constant spectral gap at high temperature for their coherent construction. Our work instead retains the accept/reject-with-rewinding architecture of \cite{Temme_2011}. In exchange we obtain approximate rather than exact Gibbs stationarity, with a polynomial precision overhead (Corollary~\ref{corollary:polycost}), and we do not compare the mixing times of the two constructions.

In this work we resurrect the Quantum Metropolis algorithm by navigating around this no-go theorem. We introduce a Boosted State-Independent Quantum Phase Estimation (BSI-QPE) protocol which ensures the magnitude of the shift in the ancilla register is close to the true energy with the sign of this shift recorded in an additional flag qubit:
\begin{multline}
  \ket{\psi_j}\ket{p}\ket{f} \;\mapsto\; \sum_{s,f'} \alpha_{f'f}(E_j,s)\,
  \ket{\psi_j}\ket{p+(-1)^{f'}s}\ket{f'},\\
  \qquad \sum_{|s-\tilde E_j|_{2^{\eta}} > \mu} \big|\alpha_{f'f}(E_j,s)\big| = O(e^{-\nu\mu}).
\end{multline}
This protocol is implementable and can fulfill the same role as the previous impossible protocol, with the modification that whenever we previously controlled off the value of the energy register we now must also control off the flag qubit. Our construction further differs from the impossible protocol by having exponentially suppressed tails rather than zero tails, which we carefully account for in our proof work.

Our main contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Circuit level construction of BSI-QPE} (Section~\ref{section:boostedphaseestimation}): Standard methods  \cite{babbush2018encoding, rendon2022effects, berry2024analyzing, greenaway2024case, patel2026optimal} for boosting QPE require a input ancilla state specially prepared to have amplitudes given by a window function. Here we propose a novel construction which boosts QPE independent of the input ancilla state with the window functions instead being introduced by a controlled single qubit $R_Y$ rotation. We show that unboosted QPE is insufficient for our later proof of approximate quantum detailed balance without a promise that energies are exactly representable in $\eta$ bits. Our novel BSI-QPE protocol is an important invention in its own right, and should have far reaching applications in other quantum algorithms which require coherent phase estimation (e.g. HHL \cite{harrow2009quantum}).
    \item \textbf{Complementary window functions} (Appendix~\ref{section:appendix}): Our construction of BSI-QPE requires a complementary pair of window functions that satisfy $\alpha(x)^2 + \beta(x)^2 =1$ and both have exponentially decaying fourier coefficients. None of the standard  window functions (e.g. DPSS, Kaiser window, Gaussian) are close to satisfying this form. We have found a novel pair of window functions that satisfy these requirements which we demonstrate numerically and prove analytically.
    \item \textbf{Elementary analysis of rewinding} (Section~\ref{section:rejection}): We provide an alternative explanation of the rewinding procedure to the Jordan's-lemma treatment of \cite{Temme_2011} which we find more transparent.
    \item \textbf{Approximate Quantum Detailed Balance Condition} (Section~\ref{section:detailedbalancedef}): We introduce a new branch-wise KMS-inspired detailed balance criteria. This allows us to consider the additive errors introduced by the exponentially suppressed (but non-zero) tails of our BSI-QPE protocol that were neglected in the original treatment \cite{Temme_2011}. We later use this condition to establish that the algorithm approximately preserves the Gibbs state.
    \item \textbf{Approximate Quantum Detailed Balance Proof} (Section~\ref{section:proofmain}): We introduce a novel \textbf{truncate} step to the proof which bounds the additive error due to the non-zero tails of BSI-QPE. We carefully convert the modular norm of the energy approximations to a standard norm \eqref{eq:condJ}-\eqref{eq:kappaclose}. We provide a novel and elegant proof (Lemma~\ref{lemma:summed-history-reversal}) that the measurement outcomes during the rejection procedure can be reversed. Further, we derive an error term \eqref{eq:Wdrifterror} overlooked in \cite{Temme_2011} that is due to the QPE energy register not being exactly returned to $\ket{0}$.
    \item \textbf{Main result} (Theorem~\ref{theorem:main}): Let $\mathcal{E}$ be one  update step of the Quantum Metropolis algorithm run with $\eta$ ancilla qubits in the energy register and a budget of at most $n^*$ rewinding attempts. Under the conditions of Theorem 1, the fixed point $\sigma^*$ of $\mathcal{E}$ is close to the Gibbs state $\sigma_G$ in trace norm $\|\cdot\|_*$:
    \begin{equation}
       \|\sigma_G- \sigma^*\|_* = O\left(\frac{\max\left( n^{*\frac{7}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\|H\| }{2^{\eta}}}\log^{\frac{3}{2}}n^*,\frac{1}{n^*}\right)}{1-k}\right)
    \end{equation}
    where $k$ is the ergodicity coefficient of $\mathcal{E}$.
\end{enumerate}
Theorem~\ref{theorem:main} restores the approximate Gibbs-invariance result of the original framework \cite{Temme_2011}, as before this does not directly prove rapid convergence which depends on the mixing properties of $\mathcal{E}$. Proving polynomial time convergence for a general Hamiltonian is likely infeasible as it would allow the solution of QMA-complete problems in polynomial time \cite{kempe2006complexity}. However, under the assumption of strict trace-norm contractivity taken in \cite{Temme_2011} we prove exponentially fast convergence to the fixed point.


The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{section:boostedphaseestimation} presents the detailed construction of our Boosted State-Independent Quantum Phase Estimation (BSI-QPE) protocol, including a detailed circuit-level description and error analysis based on window functions (extended by Appendix~\ref{section:appendix}). Section \ref{section:algorithm} describes our modified Quantum Metropolis algorithm incorporating our improved quantum phase estimation; Fig.~\ref{fig:quantum-metropolis-flowchart} provides an complete overview to help guide the reader. Section~\ref{section:why} presents the main theorem of the paper, defines our approximate quantum detailed balance framework, and outlines the four-step proof strategy applied to each branch; the full proofs are given in Appendices~\ref{appendix:Aproof} and \ref{appendix:Bproof}. Finally, Section \ref{section:discussion} discusses the broader implications of our results, the assumptions made, and the outlook for implementing these techniques on near-term or fault-tolerant quantum hardware.


\section{Boosted State-Independent Quantum Phase Estimation}
\label{section:boostedphaseestimation}

Given Hamiltonian $H$ with eigenvectors $\ket{\psi_j}$ such that $H\ket{\psi_j} = E_j \ket{\psi_j}$, quantum phase estimation (QPE) allows one to prepare an ancilla register that approximates $E_j$. Unfortunately, quantum computers have limited precision. As a result, unless the energy can be represented exactly in binary using $\eta$ bits (corresponding to $\eta$ ancilla qubits), the estimation amplitudes have slowly decaying ``fat tails'', leaving weight on estimates far from the true energy \cite{Nielsen_Chuang_2010}.

Often this issue can be mitigated by simply repeating the calculation and taking an average. However, quantum metropolis sampling requires the estimate to be available for later coherent operations, and we must be able to ``rewind" upon rejecting a proposed update. Therefore, we require a coherent method for ``boosting" QPE. 

Standard methods \cite{babbush2018encoding, rendon2022effects, berry2024analyzing, greenaway2024case, patel2026optimal} for coherently boosting QPE rely upon choosing specific input ancillary states\footnote{It is also possible to coherently boost QPE through the medians trick \cite{nagaj2009fast} used in the original Quantum Metropolis work \cite{Temme_2011}, however, as previously discussed this is insufficent as it fails to be ``shift-invariant".}. They perform QPE using ancillas initialised to the state $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{\eta}}}\sum_x \alpha(x) \ket{x}$ for some window function $\alpha(x)$ instead of the standard uniform superposition. After performing QPE upon eigenstate $\ket{\psi_j}$ the following state is produced:
\begin{equation}
   \frac{1}{2^{\eta}} \sum_x \sum_k e^{\frac{2 \pi i}{2^{\eta}}x(k-E_j)} \alpha(x)\ket{k}\ket{\psi_j}
\end{equation}
which can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_k \hat\alpha(k-E_j) \ket{k}\ket{\psi_j}
\end{equation}
where $\hat \alpha(k)$ is the discrete Fourier transform of $\alpha(x)$. By choosing $\alpha$ to be a window function one can ensure that $\hat \alpha$ is narrowly peaked, thus QPE is boosted. Unfortunately, this is not sufficient for the quantum Metropolis algorithm as we require the QPE to be independent of the ancillary state, as we must reuse the same ancillas throughout the rewinding process. 

Our BSI-QPE protocol achieves coherent boosting without requiring a specially prepared input ancilla state. It adds or subtracts an energy estimate from the existing register value, recording the direction in a flag qubit, with exponentially suppressed estimation tails as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:Gshift}.

Our construction uses two window functions $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x) = \sqrt{1-\alpha(x)^2}$ (for $x\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{\eta}-1\}$) which are both narrowly peaked in the Fourier domain. We introduce these windows via the following multi-controlled single-qubit rotation:
\begin{equation}
   CR_Y = \sum_k \ket{k}\bra{k}\otimes \begin{pmatrix}
        \alpha(k)& \beta(k)\\
        -\beta(k)& \alpha(k)
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

We also require the following time evolution gadget which is constructed as shown in Fig~\ref{fig:CRZ}:
\begin{equation}
    CR_Z =\sum_{j,k}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}\otimes\ket{k}\bra{k} \otimes \begin{pmatrix}
        e^{\frac{iE_jkt}{2}}&0\\
        0 & e^{-\frac{iE_jkt}{2}}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\begin{widetext}
BSI-QPE is built from the $R_ZR_YR_Z$ (Euler) decomposition of a single-qubit unitary~\cite{Nielsen_Chuang_2010}, which in the convention above reads
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:euler}
R_Z(\phi_1)R_Y(\theta)R_Z(\phi_2) = \begin{pmatrix}
\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i(\phi_1+\phi_2)/2} & \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i(\phi_1-\phi_2)/2}\\
-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{-i(\phi_1-\phi_2)/2} & \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{-i(\phi_1+\phi_2)/2}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The circuit in Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit} implements this with angles that depend on the ancilla value $k$. The $X$ gates on the last ancilla qubit map $k\to k' = k+2^{\eta-1} \bmod 2^{\eta}$, so the first dashed block implements $R_Z[E_jt(k-k')]$ and the second implements $R_Z[E_jt(k+k'-2^{\eta})]$, where the $-2^{\eta}$ comes from the $F$-controlled $U^{\pm 2^{\eta}}$. Substituting
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:angles}
\theta = 2\arccos\alpha(k),\qquad \phi_1 = E_jt(k+k'-2^{\eta}),\qquad \phi_2 = E_jt(k-k')
\end{equation}
into Eq.~\eqref{eq:euler}, and using $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}=\alpha(k)$, $\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\beta(k)$, gives
\begin{equation}
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha(k)e^{iE_jtk}e^{-iE_jt2^{\eta-1}} & \beta(k)e^{iE_jt k'}e^{-iE_jt2^{\eta-1}}\\
-\beta(k)e^{-iE_jt k'}e^{iE_jt2^{\eta-1}} & \alpha(k)e^{-iE_jtk}e^{iE_jt2^{\eta-1}}
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Hence, summing over $k$ and the QFTs, we get the following operation:
\begin{multline}
        \text{BSI-QPE} = \frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\sum_j\sum_{p,q,k} \begin{pmatrix}
        \alpha(k)e^{iE_jtk}e^{-iE_jt2^{\eta-1}} && \beta(k)e^{iE_jt[(k+2^{\eta-1})\text{mod} 2^{\eta}]}e^{-iE_jt2^{\eta-1}}\\
        -\beta (k)e^{-iE_jt[(k+2^{\eta-1})\text{mod} 2^{\eta}]}e^{iE_jt2^{\eta-1}} && \alpha(k)e^{-iE_jtk}e^{iE_jt2^{\eta-1}}
    \end{pmatrix}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}k(q-p)} \\\otimes\ket{p}\bra{q}\otimes\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}
\end{multline}
This can be simplified to:
\begin{equation}
    \text{BSI-QPE} = \sum_j\sum_{p,q} \begin{pmatrix}
        (-1)^{q-p}\hat\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && \hat\beta(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)\\
        -\hat\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && (-1)^{q-p}\hat\alpha(q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)
    \end{pmatrix}\otimes\ket{p}\bra{q}\otimes\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}
\end{equation}
% \begin{multline}
%   \sum_j \sum^{2^{\eta}}_{p,q} \hat \alpha(p - q - \frac{2^{\eta} t}{2\pi}E_j)\ket{0}\bra{0} \otimes\ket{p}\bra{q} \otimes \ket{\psi_j} \bra{\psi_j}\\
%   + \ket{0}\bra{0} \otimes\bm{\varepsilon}^T_{kt}
% \end{multline}
where $\hat \alpha$ is the discrete Fourier transform of $\alpha$ as given by:
\begin{equation}
   \hat\alpha(x) =\frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\sum_{k=-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}-1} \alpha(k+2^{\eta-1})e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}
\end{equation}
and $\hat \beta$ is the discrete Fourier transform of $\beta$ given by:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\beta(x) =\frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\sum_{k=-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}-1} \beta(k)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}kx}
\end{equation}
 where $\beta(k)$ is extended by $\beta(k) = \beta(k \mod 2^{\eta})$. 
 \begin{remark}[Why the Fourier transforms use different origins]
     The definition of $\hat\alpha$ shifts the window $\alpha$ by half a period, whereas the definition of $\hat\beta$ does not. This allows each window to be small at the boundaries of its own Fourier summation interval. Using the same origin for both would prevent this, because $\alpha^2+\beta^2=1$. For off-grid energies, the resulting boundary mismatch would introduce slowly decaying tails. The different origins are therefore essential to obtaining exponential tail suppression for both branches.
 \end{remark}
Throughout the rest of this work, we will use the following notational shorthand: 
\begin{equation}
    G_j^{pq} = \begin{pmatrix}
        (-1)^{q-p}\hat\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && \hat\beta(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)\\
        -\hat\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && (-1)^{q-p}\hat\alpha(q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
This shows that BSI-QPE shifts the energy-index register by approximately $\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt$, in a direction given by the qubit $F$ after the application as demonstrated by Fig.~\ref{fig:Gshift}. 

\end{widetext}
There are many potential candidates for window functions $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x)$. In this work we choose to work with $\alpha(x) = \text{sech}( r\cos^{2b} \frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}})$ and $\beta(x) = \text{tanh}(r \cos^{2b} \frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}})$. These can both be shown to have discrete Fourier transforms which decay exponentially fast as we discuss in Appendix~\ref{section:appendix}:
\begin{equation}
    |\hat \alpha(k)|,|\hat \beta(k)| = O\left( e^{-\nu |k|}\right)
    \label{eq:scaling}
\end{equation}
where $\nu = \frac{1}{3}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$. Hence we can write:
\begin{equation}
    \hat\alpha(k) = \tilde\alpha(k) + \Delta_{\alpha}(k) \>\>\>\>\>\>\hat\beta(k) = \tilde\beta(k) + \Delta_{\beta}(k)
    \label{eq:decomposealpha}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
    \tilde\alpha(k) &= \hat\alpha(k)\mathbbm{1}(|k|_{2^{\eta}}\leq \mu)\\
    \tilde\beta(k) &= \hat\beta(k)\mathbbm{1}(|k|_{2^{\eta}}\leq \mu)
    \label{eq:decomposealpha2}
\end{align}  and
\begin{equation}
    \sum_k|\Delta_\alpha(k)|,\sum_k|\Delta_\beta(k)|\leq \epsilon_{\Delta}
    \label{eq:decomposealpha3}
\end{equation} where $\epsilon_{\Delta} = O(e^{-\nu \mu})$ and $|x|_N = \min(x \mod N,- x\mod N)$ is the modular norm value of $x$.

\begin{lemma}[Band-limited BSI-QPE]
\label{lemma:0}
    Define $\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}$ as given by:
    \begin{equation}
       \widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}= \sum_j \sum_{p,q} \tilde G^{pq}_j \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q} \otimes \ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}
    \end{equation}
    where 
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde G^{pq}_j = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}
        (-1)^{q-p}\tilde\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && \tilde\beta(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)\\
        -\tilde\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && (-1)^{q-p}\tilde\alpha(q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)
    \end{smallmatrix}\right)
    \end{equation}
    Then,  
    \begin{equation}
        \|\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}-\text{BSI-QPE}\| \leq 2\epsilon_{\Delta}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
Proof is given in Appendix~\ref{section:appendixTrunc}. This shows that our BSI-QPE protocol is very close in operator norm to a fully band-limited BSI-QPE.

\begin{remark}[Why boosting is necessary]
Instead one could attempt to use unboosted state-independent QPE. This would correspond to choosing $\alpha(x)=1$, which simplifies Fig.~\ref{fig:circuit} to Fig.~\ref{fig:standard_qpe}. As this effectively acts as a rectangular window function, $|\hat\alpha(k)| = O(\frac{1}{k})$ for off-grid $k$, so $\epsilon_{\Delta} = O(\log \frac{2^{\eta}}{\mu})$ (compared to $\epsilon_{\Delta} = O(2^{-\nu \mu})$ for BSI-QPE). This bound exceeds $1$ and so unboosted state-independent QPE is insufficient for Quantum Metropolis sampling without the promise of energies exactly represented in $\eta$ bits.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Summary of BSI-QPE}

BSI-QPE shifts the value stored in the energy register by approximately the magnitude of the system's energy, with the sign of the shift recorded in the flag qubit $F$. Hence, for an $\eta$-qubit register initially holding $q$ the output amplitudes are concentrated around
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
q+\frac{2^\eta}{2\pi}E_jt
    &\quad\text{for }F=\ket{0},\\
q-\frac{2^\eta}{2\pi}E_jt
    &\quad\text{for }F=\ket{1}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where the arithmetic is modulo $2^\eta$. Figure~\ref{fig:Gshift} illustrates this action.\\

Using the window functions constructed above, both branches have exponentially suppressed tails. Changing $q$ simply translates the output, and no specially prepared initial state of the energy register or flag qubit is required. The energy eigenstate $\ket{\psi_j}$ is unchanged. 
\end{minipage}%

\section{Algorithm}
\label{section:algorithm}
Quantum metropolis is heavily inspired by the classical metropolis-hastings algorithm. The principal idea is to apply a randomly chosen update to the current state then accept/reject this update with a probability given by a function ($\tilde{f}_{E_aE_b}=\min(1, e^{-\beta(E_b-E_a)})$) of the energy before $(E_a)$ and after $(E_b)$ the update. This update process is repeated many times with the system quickly converging to equilibrium. To replicate this on a quantum computer we need to be able to: measure the energy of a state (Section~\ref{section:energymeasurement}), apply some update (Section~\ref{section:update}), accept/reject an update with the correct probability (Section~\ref{section:acceptprob}) and ``undo" an update upon deciding to reject it (Section~\ref{section:rejection}). Figure~\ref{fig:quantum-metropolis-flowchart} illustrates this full algorithm for a single update.


Throughout one full update, let $R$ denote the $\eta$-qubit BSI-QPE energy-index register and qubit $F$ the additional qubit on which the $G_j^{pq}$ act. At the start of the update, $R$ is initialised to $\ket{0}$ whilst $F$ is in the maximally mixed state (by classically choosing a random initial state $\ket{0}$ or $\ket{1}$) and the qubit $M$ on which the acceptance rotation $W_{abde}$ acts is initialized in $\ket{0}$.

\subsection{Update}
\label{section:update}
After measuring the initial energy we must apply an update to our state. In quantum metropolis this update comes in the form of a unitary operator $C$ chosen from some set $\mathcal{C}$ according to some probability measure $d\mu(C)$. It is essential for the purposes of detailed balance that the probability of choosing $C \in \mathcal{C}$ is equal to the probability of choosing $C^{\dagger} \in \mathcal{C}$ (which is alternatively expressed as $d\mu(C) = d\mu(C^{\dagger})$).
\subsection{Accept or Reject}
\label{section:acceptprob}
After applying our unitary update $C$ we must decide whether to accept or reject this update. To ensure the probability of acceptance is approximately $\tilde{f}_{E_aE_b}$, we use a combination of phase estimation, controlled rotations and single-qubit measurement (illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:Qcircuit}). First we apply phase estimation to the same registers $R, F$ used by $E_{ae}$. On the joint BSI-QPE space this operation is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j,b,p}G_j^{bp}\otimes\ket{b}\bra{p}\otimes\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}
\end{equation}
Then, using the known initial energy estimate results $a$ and $e$, we perform a $W_{abde}$ rotation on $M$, which was initialized to $\ket{0}$ at the beginning of the update, controlled by $b$ from $R$ and $d$ from $F$:
\begin{equation}
    W_{abde} = \begin{pmatrix}
        \sqrt{1 - f_{abde}} & \sqrt{f_{abde}}\\
       \sqrt{f_{abde}} & -\sqrt{1 - f_{abde}}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
Therefore, upon measuring this ancilla qubit the probability of measuring $s=1$ is given by $f_{abde}$, which we choose to approximate $\tilde f_{E_aE_b}$. This alone is insufficient to prove that quantum Metropolis converges to a Gibbs state, but it highlights the similarity with classical Metropolis. In Section~\ref{section:why} we analyse convergence rigorously.

If we measure $s=0$ (reject), then we proceed by applying $W_{abde}^{\dagger}$ ($=W_{abde}$) again after the measurement before applying inverse phase estimation and $C^{\dagger}$, to make the whole sequence ($Q_s^{ae}$) a projective binary measurement:
\begin{multline} 
    Q_s^{ae} = \sum_{j,p,q,b,d}  C^{\dagger}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}C \otimes \ket{q}\bra{p} \otimes\\ G_j^{bq\dagger}\ket{d}\bra{d}G_j^{bp}\otimes R^{abde}_s
\end{multline}
where $R^{abde}_s = W_{abde}\ket{s}\bra{s}W_{abde}$. If instead we measure $s=1$ (accept) we simply measure the energy register and then apply inverse BSI-QPE. We represent this accept process as $L^{ade}_b$ as defined in Fig.~\ref{fig:LQ_circuit} and gives the following operator:
\begin{multline}
    L^{ade}_b =\sum_{j,p,q}\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}C \otimes \ket{q}\bra{p} \otimes\\ G^{bq\dagger}_j \ket{d}\bra{d}G^{bp}_j \otimes \ket{1}\bra{1}W_{abde}
\end{multline}

The $R,F$ registers in the first $Q_s^{ae}$ measurement are the same registers used in $E_{ae}$, not separately initialized registers. Every later $P_s^{ae}$ and $Q_s^{ae}$ measurement in the update reuses these registers without resetting them. The acceptance-rotation qubit $M$ is initialized to $\ket{0}$ once and is similarly reused in every $Q_s^{ae}$. This common-register structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fullcircuit} and is required for the Marriott--Watrous rewinding argument below.

\subsection{Rejection process}
Upon rejection in the classical algorithm we can simply revert back to an earlier copy we made of the initial state or we may be able to reverse the update applied. However, in the quantum setting the no-cloning principle rules out the former and the irreversibility of measurement rules out the latter. Therefore, we need a new technique to return to our initial state.

We use an iterative sequence of binary projective measurements similar to the QMA verification amplification of Marriott and Watrous \cite{MarriottWatrous}. The first measurement $Q_s^{ae}$ has already been introduced, whilst the second $P_s^{ae}$ simply reports whether the state has returned to the initial energy:


\begin{equation}
    P^{ae}_1 = \sum_{j,p,q} \left( \ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j} \otimes \ket{q}\bra{p} \otimes G_j^{aq\dagger}\ket{e}\bra{e}G_j^{ap} \right)
\end{equation}
\begin{multline}
    P^{ae}_0 = \sum_{j,p,q} \sum_{b}\bigg( \ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j} \otimes \ket{q}\bra{p} \otimes \\G_j^{bq\dagger}(\mathbb{I} - \delta_{ab}\ket{e}\bra{e})G_j^{bp}  \bigg)
\end{multline}
This measurement is conducted as demonstrated by Fig.~\ref{fig:Pcircuit}. The register on which $P_s^{ae}$ acts is the same persistent register $R$ used by $E_{ae}$ and $Q_s^{ae}$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fullcircuit}; $P_s^{ae}$ acts trivially on the rotation qubit $M$.

The iterative process proceeds as follows after measuring $s=0$ (reject) from the initial $Q_s^{ae}$ measurement: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Measure $P_s^{ae}$
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item If $s=1$ \textbf{terminate} as the state has returned to the approximate energy of the initial state.
            \item If $s=0$ continue.
        \end{enumerate}
    \item Measure $Q_s^{ae}$
    \item Repeat up to a maximum of $n^*$ iterations
\end{enumerate}
The two measurements $P^{ae}_s$ and $Q^{ae}_s$ do not scatter the state over the whole Hilbert space. They confine it to a family of mutually orthogonal \emph{two-dimensional} subspaces, and within any one of them the state simply ping-pongs between two pairs of vectors, $\ket{\gamma_0},\ket{\gamma_1}$ and $\ket{\delta_0},\ket{\delta_1}$, according to the four-state Markov chain of Fig.~\ref{fig:transitions}. Reaching $\ket{\gamma_1}$ means the rewind has succeeded and the state has returned to its original energy. Because the chain is this simple, the probability that $n^*$ attempts all fail can be read off directly, which is what Eq.~\eqref{eq:probabilty_of_rejection} does. 

To see this let us consider the action of the rejection process on each eigenvector $\ket{\psi_j}$ of $H$. To handle the mixed initial state of $F$, purify $\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}$ as $\ket{\Phi}_{FF'}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_f\ket{f}_F\ket{f}_{F'}$, where the reference $F'$ is never acted upon. Conditioned on the outcome $(a,e)$, the corresponding pure post-measurement state is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{(E_{ae}\otimes\mathbb I_{F'})\ket{\psi_j}\ket{0}_R\ket{\Phi}_{FF'}\ket{0}_M}
    {\|(E_{ae}\otimes\mathbb I_{F'})\ket{\psi_j}\ket{0}_R\ket{\Phi}_{FF'}\ket{0}_M\|}
    = \sum_{\lambda} c_{\lambda}^{aej}\ket{\gamma_1^{ae \lambda}}.
\end{equation}
Tracing out $F'$ recovers the physical evolution from $\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2}$. Because $P_1^{ae}E_{ae}=E_{ae}$, this state is a $+1$ eigenstate of $P_1^{ae}$ (with all projectors implicitly extended by $\mathbb I_{F'}$). Furthermore, $P_1^{ae}$ commutes with $P^{ae}_1Q^{ae}_0P^{ae}_1$, so the two operators are jointly diagonalizable and the expansion above may be taken in their joint eigenstates. The untouched reference is absorbed into the degeneracy label $\lambda$.
Writing the eigenvalue of $\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}$ as $1-p^{ae\lambda}$, we have: \begin{align}1-p^{ae\lambda}&=\bra{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}P^{ae}_1Q^{ae}_0P^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1} \\&=\|Q^{ae}_0P^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}\|^2\\ &= 1-\|Q^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}\|^2
\end{align}
so $p^{ae\lambda} = \|Q^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}\|^2$, and the probability of accepting the update is given by the mixture $p_{aej} = \sum_{\lambda}|c^{aej}_{\lambda}|^2p^{ae\lambda}$. Therefore, for each $\lambda$ with $p^{ae\lambda} \in (0,1)$ we can define the following 2D invariant subspace (we have suppressed the $ae\lambda$ labels for the remainder of this section for clarity, writing $\ket{\gamma_1} = \ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}$ and $p = p^{ae\lambda}$):
\begin{align}
    \ket{\delta_0} &= \frac{Q^{ae}_0P^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma_1}}{\sqrt{1-p}} &\ket{\delta_1} &= \frac{Q^{ae}_1P^{ae}_1\ket{\gamma_1}}{\sqrt{p}} \\
    \ket{\gamma_0} &= \frac{P^{ae}_0Q^{ae}_0\ket{\delta_0}}{\sqrt{p}} &\ket{\gamma_1} &= \frac{P^{ae}_1Q^{ae}_0\ket{\delta_0}}{\sqrt{1-p}}
\end{align}
From these definitions the following relations can be deduced:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\label{eq:deltagamma0}
    P^{ae}_1 \ket{\gamma_1} &= (Q^{ae}_0+Q^{ae}_1)P^{ae}_1 \ket{\gamma_1}\\
    &= Q^{ae}_0P^{ae}_1 \ket{\gamma_1}+Q^{ae}_1P^{ae}_1 \ket{\gamma_1}\\
    &= \sqrt{1-p} \ket{\delta_0} + \sqrt{p}\ket{\delta_1}\\
    Q^{ae}_0\ket{\delta_0} &= \sqrt{p} \ket{\gamma_0} + \sqrt{1-p} \ket{\gamma_1}
\end{split}
\end{align}
As $P^{ae}_1 \ket{\gamma_1} = \ket{\gamma_1}$ and $Q^{ae}_0\ket{\delta_0} = \ket{\delta_0}$ we can simplify the above and then through substitution deduce the remaining relations:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\label{eq:deltagamma1}
        \ket{\delta_1} &= -\sqrt{1-p} \ket{\gamma_0} + \sqrt{p} \ket{\gamma_1}\\
    \ket{\gamma_0} &= \sqrt{p} \ket{\delta_0} - \sqrt{1-p}\ket{\delta_1}
\end{split}
\end{align}
Therefore it is clear that each measurement transitions the state from one of $\ket{\delta_i}$ to one of $\ket{\gamma_i}$ or vice versa, whilst remaining within the same 2D invariant subspace. As the subspaces corresponding to different $\lambda$ are mutually orthogonal (which follows from the orthogonality of the $\ket{\gamma^{ae\lambda}_1}$ together with the relations above) and each is preserved by both measurements, the subspaces do not mix at any point during the rejection process. The probability of these transitions are trivially deduced from \eqref{eq:deltagamma0} and \eqref{eq:deltagamma1}, and can be seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:transitions}.


From this we can calculate the probability of attempting to reject and then failing the rejection process within a single invariant subspace to be $p(1-p)(p^2 +(1-p)^2)^{n^*}$. As the subspaces do not mix, the overall failure probability $p_a^{fail}$ is the corresponding mixture over subspaces (reinstating the suppressed labels):
\begin{equation}
   p_{aej}^{fail} =  \sum_{\lambda}|c^{aej}_{\lambda}|^2p^{ae\lambda}(1-p^{ae\lambda})((p^{ae\lambda})^2 +(1-p^{ae\lambda})^2)^{n^*}
\end{equation}
For all $p \in [0,1]$ we have: 
\begin{equation}
    p(1-p)(p^2 +(1-p)^2)^{n^*} \leq \frac{1}{2(n^*+1)} \left(\frac{n^*}{n^*+1}\right)^{n^*}
\end{equation}
Therefore, as $\sum_{\lambda}|c^{aej}_{\lambda}|^2=1$ we have:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:probabilty_of_rejection}
   p_{aej}^{fail} \leq \frac{1}{2(n^*+1)} \left(\frac{n^*}{n^*+1}\right)^{n^*} \leq \frac{1}{2n^*}
\end{equation}
If the rejection process fails then the whole algorithm fails irrecoverably and must be reset to the original initial state.

We have chosen to present the rewinding mechanism through this lens of projective measurements and invariant subspaces in an attempt to make the procedure more intuitive. We refer the reader to the treatment via Jordan's Lemma in the original Quantum Metropolis paper \cite{Temme_2011} for an alternative approach to reach the same conclusions.
\label{section:rejection}

\section{Why it works}
\label{section:why}
The classical Metropolis-Hastings algorithm works by constructing a Markov Chain through proposing and accepting/rejecting moves with probabilities determined by the energy difference between the initial and modified states. The transition matrix of this Markov chain is in detailed balance with respect to the Gibbs state, implying that the Gibbs state is a fixed point of the Markov chain. As Markov chain is also ergodic, it is known that it must have a single unique fixed point and converge towards it. Therefore, the Markov chain converges to the Gibbs state.

In the quantum Metropolis algorithm we similarly construct a quantum Markov chain by proposing and accepting or rejecting moves based on energy differences. Because the energy differences cannot be resolved exactly, we cannot prove exact detailed balance and instead use a notion of approximate quantum detailed balance (Theorem~\ref{theorem:quantumapproximatedeatildedbalance}) that incorporates additive errors from the nonzero, exponentially suppressed tails of BSI-QPE. We use this theorem to establish that the Gibbs state is approximately invariant. Further choosing the update set $\{C\}$ to be universal ensures ergodicity and hence the uniqueness of the fixed point \cite{Temme_2011}. The closeness of the actual fixed point to this approximately invariant point additionally depends on the mixing properties of the channel. We formalise these statements into the following main result of the paper. Informally, it says: \emph{one round of the algorithm moves the Gibbs state only a little, and the two ways it can move are both controllable --- by adding ancilla qubits, and by allowing more rewinding attempts.} Formally:

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem:main}
Let $\mathcal{E}$ be one update round of the quantum Metropolis algorithm of Section~\ref{section:algorithm}, implemented with $\eta$ ancillas, evolution time $t = \frac{1}{16\|H\|}$, rewinding cap $n^*$, an update measure satisfying $d\mu(C)=d\mu(C^{\dagger})$, and window functions satisfying the conditions of Appendix~\ref{section:appendix} with truncation bandwidth $\mu = \Omega(\frac{1}{\nu}\log n^*)$, $\mu < \frac{2^{\eta}}{96\pi}, \mu < \frac{2^{\eta-3}}{4n^*+6}$, $\mu \leq \frac{2^{\eta}t}{(n^*+2)\beta}$, $\mu \leq \frac{2^{\eta}t}{384\pi\beta(4n^*+6)}$. Then
\begin{equation}
   \|\mathcal{E}[\sigma_G] - \sigma_G\|_* = O\left(\max\left( n^{*\frac{7}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\|H\| }{2^{\eta}}}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}},\frac{1}{n^*}\right)\right).
\label{eq:theoremmain}
\end{equation}
If, in addition, $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}$ is strictly trace-norm contracting with factor $k<1$ for some integer $\ell\geq1$, then $\mathcal{E}$ has a unique fixed point $\sigma^*$, is approached exponentially fast, and $\|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_* \leq \frac{\ell}{1-k}\|\mathcal{E}[\sigma_G] - \sigma_G\|_*$ (Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound}). If moreover $\frac{\ell}{1-k}\leq\tau_0$ with $\tau_0$ independent of $\eta$, $n^*$ and the window parameters, then $\sigma_G$ is prepared to any trace-norm accuracy at polynomial cost (Corollary~\ref{corollary:polycost}).
\end{theorem}

The two terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:theoremmain} have quite different origins, and it is worth separating them before the proof. The first, $n^{*\frac{7}{2}}\sqrt{\beta\|H\|2^{-\eta}}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}}$, is the residual \emph{bias} left by an imperfect energy estimate: the acceptance probability is computed from a readout that is only accurate to within the bandwidth $\mu$, so detailed balance holds only up to a multiplicative error, and every extra rewinding attempt gives that error another chance to act. It shrinks exponentially in the number of ancilla qubits $\eta$. The second, $1/n^*$, is the probability that the rewind never terminates and the whole run must be discarded (Eq.~\eqref{eq:probabilty_of_rejection}); it shrinks as the cap $n^*$ is raised.

The proof of this theorem occupies the remainder of this section and is broken down into a number of steps. First, in Section~\ref{section:detailedbalancedef} we define our approximate detailed balance criteria which allow us to consider each branch of the original $\mathcal{E}$ CP-map separately. We then proceed in Section~\ref{section:accept} and \ref{section:rejectionbranch} to prove the acceptance and rejection branches of our update step satisfy our branch-wise approximate detailed balance criteria. In both instances we follow the same four steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Truncate.} We replace $\hat \alpha$, $\hat \beta$ by their band-limited versions $\tilde \alpha$, $\tilde \beta$ as in Lemma~\ref{lemma:0}. Then, we use Lemma~\ref{lemma:3} to bound the diamond-norm difference between the original $E^{ae}$, $P_s^{ae}$, $Q_s^{ae}$ measurements and their band-limited replacements. Using Lemma~\ref{lemma:composition} we combine these bounds on the error of each measurement into a diamond-norm bound on the difference between the full band-limited map and the original.
    \item \textbf{Support.} Band-limiting means that only a narrow set of measurement outcomes contribute. We now find that set, because the relabelling of the next step is only well defined on it. By considering the restricted support of the band-limited operations we are able to find the subset of possible measurement outcomes when acting upon a given input state.
    \item \textbf{Reverse Histories.} By relabeling the indices corresponding to the measurement outcomes we are able to (approximately) relate a branch of the adjoint map with one of the original map up to the Boltzmann factors. A branch of the adjoint map describes the round run backwards, and the relabelling is nothing more than reading the measurement record from the end to the beginning. In the acceptance branch this requires that the move distribution weights $C$ and $C^{\dagger}$ equally ($d\mu(C) = d\mu(C^{\dagger})$). For the rejection branch it additionally requires demonstrating that perturbing all of the rotations by $z$ (which has small modular norm due to the restricted support) yields a controllable error on the full map.
    \item \textbf{Reweight}. Using the restricted support of the band-limited BSI-QPE we finish relating the adjoint map with the original map by exchanging the Boltzmann factors up to a multiplicative error.
\end{enumerate}
We combine the output of Lemma~\ref{lemma:1} for the band-limited acceptance and rejection branches with the Lemma~\ref{lemma:composition} bounds on the truncation. We then feed these results into Theorem~\ref{theorem:quantumapproximatedeatildedbalance} to combine with the failure to reject branch.
\subsection{Approximate Quantum Detailed Balance}
\label{section:detailedbalancedef}
In the classical setting, detailed balance can be verified by comparing each transition with its reverse, weighted by the Gibbs probabilities. Our attempts to replicate this methodology in the quantum setting are frustrated by two obstacles. First, due to inexact quantum phase estimation, we cannot exactly resolve the energies of our states and hence cannot implement the exact transition amplitudes that would lead to quantum detailed balance. Second, some branches of the weighted adjoint map agree closely not with the corresponding branch of the original map, but with a differently labelled one. In this section we therefore present a formulation of approximate quantum detailed balance that is verified branch by branch, allows branches to be paired with differently labelled partners, and accommodates the additive errors introduced by the truncation step.

For any completely positive map $\mathcal{M}$ with Kraus decomposition $\mathcal{M}[\rho] = \sum_{j} A_{j}\rho A^{\dagger}_{j}$ and any density matrix $\sigma$, we define
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{M}_{\sigma}[\rho] &= \sum_{j} A_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho\,\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} A^{\dagger}_{j} = \mathcal{M}\big[\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\rho\,\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\big],\\
    \mathcal{M}^{\dagger}[\rho] &= \sum_j A^{\dagger}_j \rho A_j,\\
    \mathcal{M}^*_{\sigma}[\rho] &= \sum_{j} \sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\dagger}_{j}\rho A_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{M}^{\dagger}[\rho]\,\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}.
\end{align}

\begin{definition}[Approximate quantum detailed balance]
\label{def:approxdb}
A completely positive map $\mathcal{M}$ satisfies $\epsilon$-approximate quantum
detailed balance with respect to $\sigma$ if
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}] - \mathcal{M}[\sigma]\|_* \leq \epsilon.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Our strategy is to establish Definition~\ref{def:approxdb} for each acceptance and rejection branch of $\mathcal{E}$ separately. Lemma~\ref{lemma:1} compares components paired by a relabelling $c\mapsto c'$. For each input basis state, its hypothesis requires the corresponding weighted outputs to differ only by a small diagonal rescaling; its conclusion converts these multiplicative discrepancies $\epsilon_{m}$ into a trace-norm bound. The lemma is stated for two maps $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{M}'$ because, in the rejection step, we can establish this criterion only after slightly modifying the acceptance rotations in the weighted adjoint map. We bound the error introduced by this modification separately.

\begin{lemma}[Branch-wise Approximate Detailed Balance Criterion]
\label{lemma:1}
Let $\mathcal{M}$ and $\mathcal{M'}$ be a completely positive maps on $d$-dimensional density matrices admitting the same decomposition $\mathcal{M} = \sum_{c\in\mathcal{X}} \mathcal{M}_c$, $\mathcal{M'} = \sum_{c\in\mathcal{X}} \mathcal{M'}_c$ into completely positive maps $\mathcal{M}_c$ and $\mathcal{M}'_c$, and let $\sigma = \sum_j \sigma_j \ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}$ be a density matrix diagonal in the orthonormal basis $\{\ket{\psi_j}\}$. Suppose there exists a bijection $c \mapsto c'$ of $\mathcal{X}$ such that, for every $j,c,l,m$,
\begin{multline}
    \bra{\psi_m}\mathcal{M}'^*_{c'\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}]\ket{\psi_l}
    =\\(1+u^{jc}_m)(1+ u^{jc}_l)
    \bra{\psi_m}\mathcal{M}_{c\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}]\ket{\psi_l}
    \label{eq:approximate_quantum_detailed_balance_condition}
\end{multline}
where the $u^{jc}_l$ are real and satisfy $|u^{jc}_l| \leq \epsilon_m$. Then:
 \begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}'^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}[\sigma] \|_* \leq (2\epsilon_m+\epsilon_m^2)\|\mathcal{M}[\sigma]\|_* \label{eq:lemma2output}
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_*$ is trace-norm.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    By definition $\mathcal{M}[\sigma]=\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]$. By linearity and the bijectivity of $c \mapsto c'$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{M}'^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}] = \sum_{j,c}\mathcal{M}'^*_{c\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}] = \sum_{j,c}\mathcal{M}'^*_{c'\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}]
    \end{equation}
    Define $N^{jc} = \mathcal{M}_{c\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}]$ and $D^{jc}=\sum_l(1+u_l^{jc})\ket{\psi_l}\bra{\psi_l}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:approximate_quantum_detailed_balance_condition} is then the operator identity $\mathcal{M}'^*_{c'\sigma}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}]=D^{jc}N^{jc}D^{jc}$. Moreover, $N^{jc}$ is positive semidefinite as $\mathcal{M}_c$ is completely positive, so that $\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}] = \sum_{j,c}N^{jc}$. Writing $V^{jc} = D^{jc}-\mathbb{I}$, which satisfies $\|V^{jc}\|\leq\epsilon_m$, we have:
    \begin{equation}
        D^{jc}N^{jc}D^{jc} - N^{jc} = V^{jc}N^{jc} + N^{jc}V^{jc} + V^{jc}N^{jc}V^{jc}
    \end{equation}
    By H\"older's inequality each term is bounded in trace-norm as:
    \begin{equation}
        \|V^{jc}N^{jc}\|_* \leq \|V^{jc}\|\|N^{jc}\|_* \leq \epsilon_m Tr[N^{jc}]
    \end{equation}
    and similarly for the remaining terms. Therefore, by the triangle inequality:
    \begin{align}
        \|\mathcal{M}'^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}_\sigma[\mathbb{I}]\|_* &\leq \sum_{j,c}\left(\|D^{jc}N^{jc}D^{jc}-N^{jc}\|_*\right)\\
        &\leq (2\epsilon_m+\epsilon_m^2)\sum_{j,c}Tr[N^{jc}] 
    \end{align}
    Finally, as each $N^{jc}$ is positive semidefinite:
    \begin{equation}
        \sum_{j,c}Tr[N^{jc}] = Tr[\mathcal{M}_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]] = Tr[\mathcal{M}[\sigma]] = \|\mathcal{M}[\sigma]\|_*
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

We use Lemma~\ref{lemma:1} to show that our band-limited maps satisfy the branch-wise approximate detailed balance
criterion for the acceptance branch $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ and every rejection branch $\tilde{B}(n)$. We then add the additive error ($\epsilon_a$) between the original map and the band-limited map to show approximate quantum detailed balance for the acceptance and rejection branches. Finally, we combine these using Theorem~\ref{theorem:quantumapproximatedeatildedbalance} to show the Gibbs state is approximately stationary.
\begin{theorem}[Approximate detailed balance implies approximate stationarity]
\label{theorem:quantumapproximatedeatildedbalance}
Let $\mathcal{E} = \sum_j \mathcal{M}_j$ be a completely positive trace-preserving map on $d$-dimensional density matrices, with each $\mathcal{M}_j$ completely positive, and let $\sigma$ be a density matrix. If each $\mathcal{M}_j$ satisfies $\epsilon^j$-approximate quantum detailed balance with respect to $\sigma$, then
\begin{equation}
     \|\mathcal{E}[\sigma]-\sigma\|_* \leq \sum_j\min\left(\epsilon^j, 2\|\mathcal{M}_j[\sigma]\|_*\right)
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    By the triangle inequality we have:
    \begin{equation}
        \|\mathcal{E}^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{E}[\sigma]\|_* \leq \sum_j \|\mathcal{M}^*_{j\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_*
    \end{equation}
    As $\mathcal{E}$ is trace-preserving, we know that:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{E}^*_{\sigma}[\mathbb{I}] = \sum_{j} \sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}A^{\dagger}_{j} A_{j}\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} = \sigma
    \end{equation}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation}
        \|\sigma - \mathcal{E}[\sigma]\|_* \leq \sum_j \|\mathcal{M}^*_{j\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_*
    \end{equation}
    By assumption we have that for all $j$:
    \begin{equation}
        \|\mathcal{M}^*_{j\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_* \leq \epsilon^j
    \end{equation}
    Further, as both
$\mathcal{M}_{j,\sigma}^{*}[\mathbb{I}]$ and
$\mathcal{M}_j[\sigma]$ are positive semidefinite:
    \begin{align}
        \|\mathcal{M}^*_{j\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]\|_* &= Tr\left[\sum_l \sigma^{\frac{1}{2}} K_{jl}^{\dagger} \mathbb{I} K_{jl} \sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]\\
        &= Tr\left[\sum_l K_{jl}\sigma K_{jl}^{\dagger}\right]\\
        &= \|\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_*
    \end{align}
    Therefore,
    \begin{equation}
         \|\mathcal{M}^*_{j\sigma}[\mathbb{I}]-\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_* \leq 2\|\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_*
    \end{equation}
    Hence,
    \begin{equation}
        \|\sigma-\mathcal{E}[\sigma]\|_* \leq \sum_j \min \left(\epsilon^j, 2\|\mathcal{M}_{j}[\sigma]\|_*\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Acceptance Branch}
\label{section:accept}
In Appendix~\ref{appendix:Aproof} we prove that:
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{A}^*_{\sigma_G}[\mathbb{I}] - \mathcal{A}[\sigma_G]\|_* = O\!\left(\frac{\beta\mu}{2^{\eta}t}\right)\|\mathcal{A}[\sigma_G]\|_* + O\!\left(\epsilon_{\Delta}\right)
    \label{eq:suma0}
\end{equation}
This is accomplished through the following high level steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Truncate.} We substitute $\mathcal{A}$ with its band-limited version $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$. Using Lemmas~\ref{lemma:3} and \ref{lemma:composition} we demonstrate that this incurs an error in trace-norm of:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \|\mathcal{A}^*_{\sigma_G}[\mathbb{I}] - \tilde{\mathcal{A}}^*_{\sigma_G}[\mathbb{I}]\|_* &\leq 24e\epsilon_{\Delta}\\
    \|\mathcal{A}[\sigma_G] - \tilde{\mathcal{A}}[\sigma_G]\|_*  &\leq 24e\epsilon_{\Delta}
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:suma1}
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Support.} We evaluate the restricted support of measurement outcomes in the band-limited map $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \left|(-1)^db -(-1)^ea-\frac{2^{\eta}t}{2\pi}(E_m-E_j)\right|_{2^{\eta}}&\leq 6\mu,\\
    \left|(-1)^db -(-1)^ea-\frac{2^{\eta}t}{2\pi}(E_l-E_k)\right|_{2^{\eta}}&\leq 6\mu,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We define $\kappa_{abde} \equiv (-1)^db -(-1)^ea \mod 2^{\eta}$ with $|\kappa_{abde}| \leq 2^{\eta-1}$, and $f_{abde} =  \min(1,e^{-\beta\frac{2\pi}{2^{\eta}t}\kappa_{abde}})$. Thus, we are able to show that upon this restricted support:
\begin{multline}
e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}(E_l+E_m)}f_{b+2^{\eta}-p_2,a+2^{\eta}-p_2,e,d}
=\\(1+u_j^{m,abdep_2})(1+u_k^{l,abdep_2})e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}(E_j+E_k)}f_{abde}
\end{multline}
where $|u^{m,abde}_j| = O(\frac{\beta \mu}{2^{\eta}t})$.

\item \textbf{Reverse Histories.} We reverse our measurement histories in an attempt to reverse the map and hence relate the adjoint map back to its original. First, we exchange the indices $a \leftrightarrow b$, $e \leftrightarrow d$, $p_1 \leftrightarrow q_1$ which is exchanges the order of measurement outcomes (and the corresponding intermediate values). This does not fully reverse the map as we have asymmetric initial and final states for the ancilla register $R$ that stores the energy values. The $R$ register begins in $\ket{0}$ but is not necessarily returned to $\ket{0}$ by the end of the map but rather to $\ket{z}$. As $G^{pq}_j$ is shift-invariant (it only depends upon $p-q$), we are able to shift all of our measurement outcomes in $\tilde{\mathcal{A}^*}$ by $z$ (the final value of register $R$) which is equivalent to exchanging the input and output of register $R$. Finally, we use that the move distribution weights $C$ and $C^{\dagger}$ equally ($d\mu(C) = d\mu(C^{\dagger})$). At this stage we have an expression for a branch of $\tilde{\mathcal{A}^*}$ that is equivalent to a branch $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ up to the Boltzmann factors.
\item \textbf{Reweight.} We use the equality we rederived upon the support of $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ to finish the identification and show that
\begin{multline}
  \bra{\psi_m}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^*_{b+2^{\eta}-p_2,a+2^{\eta}-p_2,e,d,2^{\eta}-p_2,\sigma_G}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_k}]\ket{\psi_l} 
   =\\(1+u_j^{m,abdep_2})(1+u_k^{l,abdep_2})\bra{\psi_m}\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{abdep_2,\sigma_G}[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_k}]\ket{\psi_l}.
\end{multline}
We then use Lemma~\ref{lemma:1} to show that 
\begin{equation}
    \|\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^*_{\sigma_G}[\mathbb{I}] - \tilde{\mathcal{A}}[\sigma_G]\|_* \leq O\left(\frac{\beta \mu}{2^{\eta}t}\right)\|\tilde{\mathcal{A}}[\sigma_G]\|_*.
    % \label{eq:Aoutput}
    \label{eq:suma2}
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
We can then combine \eqref{eq:suma1} and \eqref{eq:suma2} to prove \eqref{eq:suma0}.

\subsubsection{Rejection branch}
\label{section:rejectionbranch}
In Appendix~\ref{appendix:Bproof} we will prove that:
\begin{multline}
    \|\mathcal{B}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\mathbb{I}] - \mathcal{B}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* = O\left(\frac{(n+2)\beta\mu}{2^{\eta}t} \right)\|\mathcal{B}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* +\\O\left(\max\left((n+2)^2
    \mu\epsilon_{\Delta},(n+2)^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta }{2^{\eta}t}}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\right) 
    \label{eq:sumb0}
\end{multline}
The proof is accomplished through the following high level steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Truncate.} Before performing truncation we first highlight that every rejection map (bar the first) is invariant under flipping the first $Q_0$ to $Q_1$. Therefore, we can replace it with an average over both measurement outcomes. This will be important later for reversing the histories as without this the histories would always begin with a $s_0=0$ and hence cannot be reversed as they can end with either $s_n=0$ or $s_n=1$.

    We then perform the same truncation as before, replacing $\mathcal{B}(n)$ with its band-limited version $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}(n)$, and using Lemmas~\ref{lemma:3} and \ref{lemma:composition} to demonstrate:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \|\mathcal{B}(n)[\sigma_G] - \tilde{\mathcal{B}}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* &\leq 24e(2n+3)\epsilon_{\Delta},
    \\ \|\mathcal{B}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\mathbb{I}] - \tilde{\mathcal{B}}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\mathbb{I}]\|_* &\leq 24e(2n+3)\epsilon_{\Delta}.       
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:sumb1}
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Support.} We expand out the expression for $\tilde{\mathcal{B^*}}_{\sigma_G}(n)$ and find the restricted support of the measurement outcomes. Using these measurement outcomes we are able to derive the following conditions for $\bra{\psi_m}\tilde{\mathcal{B}}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_k}]\ket{\psi_l} \neq 0$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \left|\frac{2^{\eta}t}{2\pi}(E_m-E_j)\right|&\leq(4n+8)\mu,\\
    \left|\frac{2^{\eta}t}{2\pi}(E_l-E_k)\right|&\leq(4n+8)\mu.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Reweight.} We use these conditions to see:
\begin{equation}
    e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}(E_l+E_m)}=(1+v_l^k)(1+v_m^j)e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}(E_j+E_k)}
\end{equation}
where $v_m^j=O(\frac{(n+2)\beta \mu}{2^{\eta}t})$. Using this equality we are able to exchange the Boltzmann factor in $\tilde{\mathcal{B^*}}_{\sigma_G}(n)$ with the addition of multiplicative errors.

\item \textbf{Reverse Histories.} We again apply a relabeling to reverse the order of the measurement outcomes/intermediate indices. By following the relabelling and applying Lemma~\ref{lemma:summed-history-reversal} we can transform expression for a branch of $\tilde{\mathcal{B^*}}_{\sigma_G}(n)$ into the form of a branch of $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{\sigma_G}(n)$ with the exception that the initial and final states of the $R$ register are exchanged (as it is not returned to $\ket{0}$ but rather to $\ket{z}$). We exchange these states by again subtracting $z$ from all of the measurement outcomes. Unfortunately, bounding the impact of this shift by $z$ upon the map is significantly more involved than in the acceptance branch. We can isolate the impact of this shift by $z$ to the $W$ rotations within each application of $Q_s$. We define a new map $\tilde{\mathcal{T}^*}(n)$ as equal to $\tilde{\mathcal{B^*}}_{\sigma_G}(n)$ but with the $W$ rotations shifted to cancel out the shift by $z$. Thus we have an expression for a branch of $\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^*(n)$ in terms of a branch of $\tilde{\mathcal{B}}(n)$:
\begin{multline}
    \bra{\psi_m}\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^*_{z,\sigma_G}(n)[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_k}]\ket{\psi_l}
    =\\(1+v_l^k)(1+v_m^j)\bra{\psi_m}\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{2^{\eta}-z,\sigma_G}(n)[\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_k}]\ket{\psi_l}.
\end{multline}
and thus by Lemma~\ref{lemma:1}
\begin{multline}
    \|\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\mathbb{I}] - \tilde{\mathcal{B}}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* \leq \\O\left(\frac{(n+2)\beta \mu}{2^{\eta}t}\right)\|\tilde{\mathcal{B}}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_*.
    \label{eq:sumb2}
\end{multline}
Using $|z|_{2^{\eta}} \leq (4n+6)\mu$ (from the \textbf{Support} step) and Lemma~\ref{lemma:wdrift} we can bound the deviation in operator norm of $W$ caused by this shift in $z$ when $(4n^*+6)\mu < 2^{\eta-3}$. We then use Lemmas~\ref{lemma:3} and \ref{lemma:composition} (when $\mu \leq \frac{2^{\eta}t}{384\pi\beta(4n^*+6)}$) to relate this operator norm bound to a diamond-norm bound on the difference between $\tilde{\mathcal{T}^*}(n)$ and $\tilde{\mathcal{B}^*}(n)$:
\begin{multline}
    \|\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^*(n) - \tilde{\mathcal{B}}^*(n)\|_{\diamond} \leq 24e(n+1)\sqrt{\beta\frac{6\pi}{2^{\eta}t}}((4n+6)\mu)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\\48e(n+1)(4n+6)\mu\epsilon_{\Delta}
    \label{eq:sumb3}
\end{multline}
\end{enumerate}
Hence combining \eqref{eq:sumb1}, \eqref{eq:sumb2} and \eqref{eq:sumb3} with the triangle inequality we can prove \eqref{eq:sumb0}.

    \label{eq:error_bound}
\end{multline}

We can consider $\mu$ to be the width of the effective energy bins needed to make our QPE protocol exact, and hence $c=\log\mu$ is the number of additional ancilla qubits required by BSI-QPE to compensate for the inexactness of QPE. Thus $c=\Omega(\log(\frac{1}{\nu}\log n^*))$.
%  \begin{equation}
%    \epsilon_c = \|\mathcal{E}[\sigma_G] - \sigma_G\|_* = O(\max(\epsilon_r,n^*\epsilon_{\Delta},2^{-\eta}\beta\mu\|H\|,2^{-\eta}\mu\|H\|))

Approximate quantum detailed balance of $\mathcal{E}$ with respect to $\sigma_G$ is not by itself enough to ensure that the fixed point of $\mathcal{E}$ is close to $\sigma_G$; this depends on the mixing properties of the channel. One sufficient, but not necessary, condition is that $\mathcal{E}$ is strictly trace-norm contracting after some number $\ell$ of rounds:
 \begin{theorem}
     If a completely positive trace preserving map $\mathcal{E}$ satisfies:
     \begin{equation}
         \|\mathcal{E}[\sigma_G] - \sigma_G\|_* \leq \epsilon
     \end{equation}
     and, for some integer $\ell\geq1$,
 \begin{equation}
     \|\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\rho-\sigma]\|_* \leq k\|\rho-\sigma\|_*
 \end{equation}
for $k \in [0,1)$. Then, we have that $\mathcal{E}$ converges to a unique fixed point $\sigma^*$ such that:
 \begin{equation}
     \| \sigma^* -\sigma_G\|_* \leq \frac{\ell\epsilon}{1-k},
     \qquad
     \|\mathcal{E}^n[\rho] -\sigma^*\|_* \leq 2k^{\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor}
 \end{equation}
 for every state $\rho$ and integer $n\geq0$.
 \label{theorem:error_bound}
 \end{theorem}
 \begin{proof}
Because $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}$ is strictly contracting and the density matrices equipped with the trace norm form a complete metric space, Banach's fixed-point theorem implies that $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}$ has a unique fixed point $\sigma^*$ \cite{Mart_nez_Pe_a_2023,Raginsky_2002}. As $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\mathcal{E}[\sigma^*]]=\mathcal{E}[\sigma^*]$, the state $\mathcal{E}[\sigma^*]$ is also fixed by $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}$, so $\mathcal{E}[\sigma^*]=\sigma^*$. Every fixed point of $\mathcal{E}$ is fixed by $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}$, so $\sigma^*$ is the unique fixed point of $\mathcal{E}$. Writing $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma_G]-\sigma_G=\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\mathcal{E}^{i}\big[\mathcal{E}[\sigma_G]-\sigma_G\big]$ and using trace-norm contractivity of $\mathcal{E}$ gives $\|\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma_G]-\sigma_G\|_*\leq\ell\epsilon$. Using $\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma^*]=\sigma^*$ and the triangle inequality,
\begin{align}
 \|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_*
 &\leq \|\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma^*]-\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma_G]\|_*+\|\mathcal{E}^{\ell}[\sigma_G]-\sigma_G\|_*\\
 &\leq k\|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_*+\ell\epsilon,
\end{align}
which gives $\|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_*\leq\ell\epsilon/(1-k)$. Moreover, the map converges to this fixed point exponentially fast:
\begin{equation}
     \|\mathcal{E}^n[\rho] -\sigma^*\|_* \leq k^{\lfloor n/\ell\rfloor}\| \rho - \sigma^*\|_*
\end{equation}
 \end{proof}
 
Combining Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_bound}, the quantum Metropolis algorithm converges exponentially fast to a unique fixed point $\sigma^*$ whose distance from the Gibbs state is bounded by
\begin{equation}
    \|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_* =
    O\left(\frac{\ell\max\left( n^{*\frac{7}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\|H\| }{2^{\eta}}}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}},\frac{1}{n^*}\right)}{1-k}\right)
\end{equation}
This establishes the second part of Theorem~\ref{theorem:main}.
 
Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound} applies to each channel separately. Since $\mathcal{E}$ depends on $\eta$, $n^*$ and the window parameters, so do $\ell$ and $k$, and reducing $\epsilon$ improves the fixed point only if $\ell/(1-k)$ does not grow at a comparable rate. A bound on $\ell/(1-k)$ that is uniform in these parameters is one sufficient, but not necessary, way to guarantee this.
\begin{corollary}
\label{corollary:polycost}
Suppose that every channel $\mathcal{E}$ in Theorem~\ref{theorem:main} satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound} with some $\ell$ and $k$ such that $\frac{\ell}{1-k}\leq\tau_0$, where $\tau_0$ is independent of $\eta$, $n^*$ and the window parameters. Then for every $0<\epsilon'<1$ there are parameters with $n^*=O(\tau_0/\epsilon')$, $2^{\eta}=O\big((1+\beta\|H\|)\mu^3(\tau_0/\epsilon')^{9}\big)$ and a number of rounds $T=\lceil\tau_0(1+\log\frac{4}{\epsilon'})\rceil$ such that $\|\mathcal{E}^T[\rho]-\sigma_G\|_*\leq\epsilon'$ for every state $\rho$, with total normalised Hamiltonian evolution time $\tilde O\big((1+\beta\|H\|)\tau_0^{11}\epsilon'^{-10}\big)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
Let $C_0$ be the constant in Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_bound}. Choose $n^*=\lceil\frac{4C_0\tau_0}{\epsilon'}\rceil$ and $\mu=\Theta(\frac{1}{\nu}\log n^*)$. Take $\eta$ to be the smallest integer with $2^{\eta}\geq\frac{16C_0^2\tau_0^2\beta\|H\|n^{*7}\mu^3}{\epsilon'^2}$ that also satisfies the remaining hypotheses of Theorem~\ref{theorem:main}. Then Eq.~\eqref{eq:error_bound} and Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound} give $\|\sigma^*-\sigma_G\|_*\leq\frac{\epsilon'}{2}$. Writing $\tau=\frac{\ell}{1-k}\leq\tau_0$ and using $k^x\leq e^{-(1-k)x}$,
\begin{equation}
    k^{\lfloor T/\ell\rfloor}\leq e^{-(1-k)(T/\ell-1)}\leq e^{1-T/\tau}\leq e^{1-T/\tau_0}\leq\frac{\epsilon'}{4},
\end{equation}
so Theorem~\ref{theorem:error_bound} gives
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{E}^T[\rho]-\sigma_G\|_*\leq 2k^{\lfloor T/\ell\rfloor}+\frac{\epsilon'}{2}\leq\epsilon'.
\end{equation}
Each round uses at most $4n^*+6$ applications of BSI-QPE or its inverse, and each application has Hamiltonian evolution time $O(2^{\eta}t)$. The total normalised evolution time is therefore $\tilde O\big((1+\beta\|H\|)\tau_0^{11}\epsilon'^{-10}\big)$, where $\tilde O$ hides factors of $\mu$ (polylogarithmic in $\tau_0/\epsilon'$). This is polynomial in $\tau_0$, $\beta\|H\|$ and $\frac{1}{\epsilon'}$, using only $\eta=O\big(\log\frac{(1+\beta\|H\|)\tau_0}{\epsilon'}\big)$ ancillas.
\end{proof}
The polynomial degree in Corollary~\ref{corollary:polycost} is an artefact of worst-case bounds and is not expected to be tight. Routes to tighter bounds might include using the $O(1)$ expected number of rewinding iterations per round (rather than the $O(n^*)$ worst case), a smoothed acceptance function, and a less conservative treatment of how errors accumulate across the rejection branches. The purpose of Corollary~\ref{corollary:polycost} is to establish that the algorithm has polynomial cost, not to provide a competitive resource estimate.
 
\section{Discussion}
\label{section:discussion}
The Quantum Metropolis algorithm was set aside as it depended on a phase
estimation primitive that was subsequently proved impossible. The no-go theorem \cite{chen2023quantumthermalstatepreparation} forbids a boosted phase estimation protocol that shifted the energy register in a definite direction, but it says nothing about one that shifts in both directions at once. BSI-QPE does exactly that, recording which direction was taken in a single additional qubit, which turns out to be sufficient. Moreover, BSI-QPE has exponentially suppressed tails rather than the zero tails assumed by the no-go theorem. The no-go theorem is not contradicted, rather the protocol it rules out is stronger than the algorithm actually requires. Hence the rewinding route of \cite{Temme_2011} is again available, alongside weak measurement \cite{jiang2024quantummetropolissamplingweak}, Lindbladian samplers and exactly detailed-balanced discrete-time constructions \cite{gilyen2024quantum}; how their costs and mixing times compare remains open.

We have shown that the Gibbs state $\sigma_G$ is an approximate fixed point of the Quantum Metropolis algorithm (which operates as a Quantum Markov chain) . Much as with classical Markov chains the rate of convergence to its fixed point is related to the spectral gap ($\Delta$) of the map. For exact quantum detailed balance it is known \cite{temme2010chi} that the trace-norm distance from the fixed point is bounded by $\sqrt{\chi^2_{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho_0,\sigma^*)} (1-\Delta)^\kappa$ for $\kappa$ consecutive applications of the map (where $\chi^2_{\frac{1}{2}}(\rho_0,\sigma^*)$ may scale exponentially in system size). Assuming we can extend this bound to approximate quantum detailed balance, we require $O(\frac{1}{\Delta}\log \frac{1}{\epsilon})$ update steps to produce a state with trace-norm distance $\epsilon$ from the fixed point. Hence, determining the scaling of convergence time with system size requires understanding the scaling of the spectral gap.

Determining the scaling of spectral gap with system size is a hard problem. For arbitrary Hamiltonians the inverse spectral gap cannot be proven to scale polynomially at all temperatures without collapsing QMA into BQP \cite{oliveira2008complexityquantumspinsystems, Aharonov_2009, Schuch_2009}, and it is a challenging problem to prove favourable scaling for realistic Hamiltonians. However, the classical setting suffers an analogous obstruction: finding ground states of Ising spin glasses is NP-complete \cite{Barahona:1982zdv} and rigorous rapid-mixing proofs exist only in restricted regimes \cite{Martinelli1999}. This has not prevented classical Metropolis-Hastings from practically succeeding for a great many complex systems of real physical interest \cite{thijssen2007computational}. We expect that in a similar fashion the practical achievements of the Quantum Metropolis algorithm will far outstrip what can be proven about it. 
 
For any system that thermalises it is expected that the Quantum Metropolis algorithm will quickly converge as the moves can mimic thermalisation. However, the true power of the quantum metropolis algorithm comes not from its potential to imitate physical thermalisation, but its ability to perform non-physical moves. In classical monte carlo it has been demonstrated \cite{blote2002cluster,todo2019parallel} that performing non-physical moves (loop/cluster algorithms) can speed up convergence by as much as 8 orders of magnitude, and comparable order-of-magnitude speed-ups may be available in the quantum setting. Support for non-physical moves, both non-local and adaptive, is therefore a major strength of the Quantum Metropolis algorithm. It has recently been shown that Lindbladian samplers can also host non-local moves \cite{chen2025efficient} so which framework is the better home for such moves remains an open question. Adaptive moves are a different matter: it has yet to be shown whether Lindbladian samplers can implement them, whereas the accept/reject Metropolis architecture is structurally well-placed to do so.

A quadratic speedup \cite{yung2012quantum} of the original Quantum Metropolis algorithm can be achieved via the use of a Szegedy-type quantum walk \cite{szegedy2004quantum}. Our modified Quantum Metropolis algorithm should be compatible with this approach and could, in principle, benefit from a similar speedup. This remains intriguing direction for future work, particularly in light of the unresolved question concerning the robustness of the speedup in the presence of imperfect phase estimation. 

Throughout our work, we have assumed perfect quantum time evolution, however, in practice there will always be some operator norm error $\epsilon^T_t$ in implementing time evolution. Future work may focus on extending our framework to explicitly include such imperfections and to characterise the robustness of our results under hardware noise models.

In this section we prove that:

\textbf{Truncate.} We begin with $\mathcal{A}$ and perform a truncation to $\tilde{\mathcal{A}}$ by substituting $\tilde \alpha, \tilde \beta$ for $\hat \alpha, \hat \beta$. Under this substitution $G^{pq}_j$ becomes $\tilde G^{pq}_j$ such that $\|\tilde G^{pq}_j\| =0$ for all $|p-q\pm\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt|_{2^{\eta}} > \mu$.  By Lemma~\ref{lemma:0} this produces a modified operator $\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}$ such that $\|\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}- \text{BSI-QPE}\| \leq 2\epsilon_{\Delta}$. In Appendix~\ref{section:appendixTrunc} we prove the following Lemma that relates an operator norm bound approximation of a Kraus operator to a diamond-norm bound in the approximation of the CP-map.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:3}
Let $\{D_s\}_{s\in S}$ be mutually orthogonal projectors satisfying $\sum_{s\in S} D_s \leq \mathbb{I}$. Given unitaries $U$ and $V$, define the measurement $K_s = U^{\dagger}D_s V$ and CP map $\mathcal{K}[\rho] = \sum_{s \in S} K_s\rho K_s^{\dagger}$. Further, given operators $\tilde U, \tilde V$, define approximate measurement operators $\tilde K_s = \tilde U^{\dagger}D_s \tilde V$ and the approximate map $\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[\rho] = \sum_{s \in S} \tilde K_s\rho \tilde K_s^{\dagger}$. If $\|U-\tilde U\| \leq \epsilon<\frac14$ and $\|V-\tilde V\| \leq \epsilon<\frac14$, then 
\begin{equation}

\end{lemma}

\end{equation}
We can combine these bounds using the following Lemma which we also prove in Appendix~\ref{section:appendixTrunc}.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:composition}
Let $\mathcal{M}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{M}_k$ be completely positive trace-non-increasing maps and let $\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1, \ldots, \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k$ be completely positive maps with $\|\mathcal{M}_i - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq \varepsilon$ for all $i$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1\|_{\diamond} \leq (1+\varepsilon)^k - 1 \leq e k \varepsilon,
\end{equation}
the last inequality holding whenever $k\varepsilon \leq 1$. More generally, if instead $\|\mathcal{M}_i\|_{\diamond}, \|\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq c$ for some $c \geq 1$ and $\|\mathcal{M}_i - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq \varepsilon_i$, without assuming the $\mathcal{M}_i$ are trace-non-increasing, then
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1\|_{\diamond} \leq c^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_i .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

when $2\sqrt{\beta\frac{6\pi}{2^{\eta}t}}\sqrt{(4n+6)\mu} \leq \frac{1}{4}$. \begin{equation}

\end{equation}
As $|z|_{2^{\eta}} \leq (4n+6)\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \|\tilde{\mathcal{T}}^*(n) - \tilde{\mathcal{B}}^*(n)\|_{\diamond} \leq 24e(n+1)\sqrt{\beta\frac{6\pi}{2^{\eta}t}}((4n+6)\mu)^{\frac{3}{2}}+48e(n+1)(4n+6)\mu\epsilon_{\Delta}
    \label{eq:Wdrifterror}
\end{equation}
Therefore, combining this with \eqref{eq:Boutput} and \eqref{eq:Bsrelation} via the triangle inequality, $\mathcal{B}(n)$ satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{theorem:quantumapproximatedeatildedbalance} as:
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{B}^*_{\sigma_G}(n)[\mathbb{I}] - \mathcal{B}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* = O\left(\frac{(n+2)\beta\mu}{2^{\eta}t} \right)\|\mathcal{B}(n)[\sigma_G]\|_* +O\left(\max\left((n+2)^2\mu\epsilon_{\Delta},(n+2)^{\frac{5}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta }{2^{\eta}t}}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}}\right)\right) 
\end{equation}
\section{Window functions}
\label{section:appendix}
There are many potential pairs of window functions that could be chosen for use in BSI-QPE. All these pairs of functions $\alpha(x), \beta(x)$ must satisfy the following characteristics for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\alpha(x)^2+\beta(x)^2 =1$
    \item $\alpha(x+2^{\eta}) = \alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x+2^{\eta}) = \beta(x)$
    \item $\sum_{|k|_{2^{\eta}}\geq\mu}|\hat \alpha(k)|, \sum_{|k|_{2^{\eta}}\geq\mu}|\hat \beta(k)| \leq \epsilon_\Delta$
\end{enumerate}
In this work we have chosen the example of $\alpha(x) = \text{sech}( r\cos^{2b} \frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}})$ and $\beta(x) = \text{tanh}(r \cos^{2b} \frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}})$ for $b \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ and $r \in \mathbb{R}^+$. These satisfy characteristics 1 and 2 directly. Below we demonstrate analytically and numerically that they satisfy characteristic 3 with $\epsilon_{\Delta}$ decaying exponentially with $\mu$.


Both $\text{sech } z$ and $\text{tanh } z$ are analytic everywhere on the complex plane except at isolated poles where $\cosh z = 0$. These poles occur at $z = i(\frac{\pi}{2} + k \pi)$ for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Therefore, as cosine is analytic we have that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are analytic everywhere except for poles where $r \cos^{2b}\frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}} = i(\frac{\pi}{2} + k \pi)$ for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, equivalently $\cos \frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}} = (\frac{\pi}{2r}|1+2k|)^{\frac{1}{2b}} e^{\frac{i\pi(\operatorname{sgn}(2k+1)+4l)}{4b}}$  for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ and all $l \in \{0,\ldots,2b-1\}$. Therefore, the poles of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ lie at $\pm\frac{2^{\eta}}{\pi}\arccos ((\frac{\pi}{2r}|1+2k|)^{\frac{1}{2b}}e^{\frac{i\pi(\operatorname{sgn}(2k+1)+4l)}{4b}}) + 2^{\eta+1} k'$ for $k, k' \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $l \in \{0,\ldots,2b-1\}$. We have that \cite[\href{http://dlmf.nist.gov/4.23.E35}{4.23.35}]{NIST:DLMF}:
\begin{equation}
    |\operatorname{Im}(\arccos z)| = \text{arccosh }{\kappa}
\end{equation}
where $z=x+iy$ and
\begin{equation}
    \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{(x+1)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-1)^2+y^2}}{2}.
\end{equation}
The poles of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ corresponding to $k$ have the following $\kappa$:
\begin{align}
    \kappa_{kl} &= \frac{\sqrt{A_k^2 + 2A_k\cos \frac{\pi(\operatorname{sgn}(2k+1)+4l)}{4b}+1} +\sqrt{A_k^2 - 2A_k\cos \frac{\pi(\operatorname{sgn}(2k+1)+4l)}{4b}+1}}{2}\\
    &= \sqrt{\frac{A_k^2+1 + \sqrt{(A_k^2-1)^2+4A_k^2\sin^2\frac{\pi(\operatorname{sgn}(2k+1)+4l)}{4b}}}{2}}
    \label{eq:kappa}
\end{align}
where $A_k = (\frac{\pi}{2r}|1+2k|)^{\frac{1}{2b}}$.  As Eq~\eqref{eq:kappa} and $\text{arcosh } \kappa$ are both monotonically increasing for $A_k, \kappa>0$, we can lower bound the distance from the real line of all the poles in $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by $\text{arcosh } \kappa_{0l}$. By choosing $b$ such that $A_0 > \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ then we have $\kappa_{0l} > 1 + \frac{1}{6}\sin^2\frac{\pi(1+4l)}{4b} \geq 1 + \frac{1}{6}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$. As $\text{arcosh } (1+x) > x$ in the range $0<x<1$, we have $\text{arcosh } \kappa_{0l} > \frac{1}{6}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$. Therefore, all the poles of $\alpha(\frac{2^{\eta}}{\pi}x)$ and $\beta(\frac{2^{\eta}}{\pi}x)$ are at least $\frac{1}{6}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$ from the real line. Hence the poles of $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x)$ are at least $\frac{2^{\eta}}{6\pi}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$ from the real line, and hence $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are analytic in some strip $S =\{z \in \mathbb{C}: |\text{Im } z| \leq \frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}\}$ with $\nu \geq \frac{1}{3}\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}$. As can be seen from Fig.~\ref{fig:scalingmu} in practice $\nu$ greatly exceeds this bound.

Therefore, as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are $2^{\eta}$-periodic, real, analytic in the strip $S =\{z \in \mathbb{C}: |\text{Im } z| \leq \frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}\}$ and $|\alpha(z)|, |\beta(z)| \leq M$ in $S$, we have that \cite{trefethen2014exponentially}:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conv1}
    \left|\hat\alpha(k) - \frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\int_{-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}}\alpha(x+2^{\eta-1})e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}kx} dx\right| \leq \frac{2 Me^{\nu |k|}}{e^{2^{\eta}\nu}-1} = O(e^{-2^{\eta-1}\nu})
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conv2}
    \left|\hat\beta(k) - \frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\int_{-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}}\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}kx} dx\right| \leq  \frac{2 Me^{\nu |k|}}{e^{2^{\eta}\nu}-1} = O(e^{-2^{\eta-1}\nu})
\end{equation}
for integer $|k| \leq 2^{\eta-1}$. Thus the discrete Fourier transforms of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ converge doubly exponentially in $\eta$ to the corresponding continuous Fourier coefficients. Further, as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are analytic in the strip $S$, we can deform the integration contour:
\begin{align}
    \left|\int_{-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}}\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}kx} dx\right| &=\left| \int_{-2^{\eta-1}\pm i\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}^{2^{\eta-1}\pm i\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}kx} dx\right|\\
    &\leq 2^{\eta}Me^{-\nu|k|}
    \label{eq:tails}
\end{align}
where the contributions from the vertical sides cancel as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are $2^{\eta}$-periodic. Combining this decay of the continuous Fourier coefficients with Eqs.~\eqref{eq:conv1} and~\eqref{eq:conv2} gives, uniformly for modes away from the Nyquist boundary,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:exactdecay}
    |\hat \alpha(k)|, |\hat \beta(k)| = O\!\left(M e^{-\nu|k|}
    +M e^{-\nu(2^{\eta}-|k|)}\right),
    \qquad \nu\geq\frac13\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4b}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, for $\mu\ll 2^{\eta}$ the second term is negligible and characteristic 3 is satisfied with $\epsilon_{\Delta}=O(e^{-\nu\mu})$.
\subsection{Non-integer energies}
If $\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_it$ is not an integer, then the discrete Fourier transform is sampled off-grid. This can be reinterpreted as the on-grid Fourier transform of a modified function:
\begin{align}
    \hat \beta(k -\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_it) &= \frac{1}{2^{\eta}} \sum_{x=-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}-1} \beta(x) e^{-iE_it x}e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}} kx}\\
    &= \frac{1}{2^{\eta}} \sum_{x=-2^{\eta-1}}^{2^{\eta-1}-1} \tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}} kx}\\
    &= \hat{\tilde\beta}(k)
\end{align}
The modified function $\tilde\beta(x)$ is neither real nor $2^{\eta}$-periodic. We therefore cannot apply Eqs.~\eqref{eq:conv1} and~\eqref{eq:conv2} directly to prove convergence of the discrete Fourier transform to the continuous Fourier transform, nor can we use Eq.~\eqref{eq:tails} without accounting for the vertical sides of the contour.

Instead we can use Theorem 5.1 from \cite{javed2014trapezoidal} to bound the midpoint-rule error for a function with continuous first derivative on the boundary of the region $R = \{z \in \mathbb{C}: |\operatorname{Im} z| \leq  \frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}, -2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\leq\operatorname{Re} z \leq 2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\}$ and analytic in its interior. We slightly modify the error term in this theorem by inserting $\tilde \beta(k+2^{\eta})- \tilde \beta(k)= (e^{-iE_i t2^{\eta}}-1)\tilde \beta(k)$ at equation (4.9) in their proof, which allows the midpoint analogue of equation (4.12) to be bounded directly by $\frac{2\epsilon_b}{2^{\eta}\pi}$ rather than having to deal with derivatives:
\begin{equation}
    \left| \hat \beta\!\left(k-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_it\right) - \frac{1}{2^{\eta}} \int_{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}^{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\,dx \right| \leq \frac{2 Me^{\nu|k-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_i t|_{2^{\eta}}}}{e^{2^{\eta}\nu}-1} + \frac{2\epsilon_{b}}{\pi\cdot 2^{\eta}}
\end{equation}
where $|\beta(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}+iy)| \leq \epsilon_{b}$ for all $-\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}<y<\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}$. Therefore, the convergence of the discrete Fourier transform to the continuous Fourier transform is only guaranteed to be exponential in $\eta$ rather than doubly exponential.

Now we can again consider the deformation of the integrals of the continuous Fourier transform; however, we can no longer assume cancellation of the vertical sides of the integral:
\begin{multline}
     \left|\int_{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}^{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\right| \leq \left|\int_{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}^{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\pm i\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\right|+\left|\int_{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\pm i\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}^{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\pm i\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\right|+\\\left|\int_{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}\pm i \frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}}^{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\right|
\end{multline}
Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    \left|\frac{1}{2^{\eta}}\int_{-2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}^{2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde\beta(x)e^{\frac{2\pi i}{2^{\eta}}xk}\right| \leq Me^{-\nu|k-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_i t|_{2^{\eta}}} + \frac{\epsilon_b}{\pi|k-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_i t|_{2^{\eta}}}
\end{equation}
where $|\beta(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac{1}{2}+iy)| \leq\epsilon_b$ for all $-\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}<y<\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}$. From this we can clearly see we need to have $\epsilon_b \ll 1$ or the decay will be dominated by the $\frac{1}{k}$ term. This is the reason why we must take the discrete fourier transform of $\alpha$ with the period shifted over by half as we must have both $\alpha, \beta \ll 1$ at the boundary of the discrete fourier transform. For
\begin{equation}
\beta(x)=\tanh\!\left(r\cos^{2b}\frac{\pi x}{2^{\eta}}\right),
\label{eq:betadefinitionvertical}
\end{equation}
one has the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\left|
\cos\frac{\pi\left(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac12+iy\right)}{2^{\eta}}
\right|^2
=
\sin^2\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}
+\sinh^2\frac{\pi y}{2^{\eta}}.
\label{eq:verticalcosineidentity}
\end{equation}
Let us choose $\nu \leq \frac{1}{2}$ such that $\sinh \frac{\nu}{2} \leq \nu$ then since $|y|\leq\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}$ and $\sin x\leq x$ we have:
\begin{equation}
\left|
\cos\frac{\pi\left(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac12+iy\right)}{2^{\eta}}
\right|^2
\leq
\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}\right)^2+\nu^2.
\label{eq:verticalcosinebound}
\end{equation}
Therefore, using $|\tanh z|\leq2|z|$ for $|z|\leq\frac12$,
\begin{equation}
\left|\beta\left(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac12+iy\right)\right|
\leq
2r\left(
\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}\right)^2+\nu^2
\right)^{b},
\label{eq:verticalbetabound}
\end{equation}
provided $r\left(
\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}\right)^2+\nu^2
\right)^{b}\leq\frac12$ for all $-\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}<y<\frac{2^{\eta}\nu}{2\pi}$.
For $\alpha$ we use the shifted window $\alpha(x+2^{\eta-1})$. Hence,
\begin{equation}
    \left|\alpha(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac12+iy+2^{\eta-1})\right| = \left|\operatorname{sech}\!\left(
r\cos^{2b}\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} +\frac{i\pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right)
\right)\right|
\end{equation}
We have:
\begin{align}
   \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} +\frac{i \pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right) &= \cos \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \cosh \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}} -i \sin \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \sinh \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}} \\
   &= \sqrt{\cos^2 \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \cosh^2 \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}}+\sin^2 \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \sinh^2 \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}} }e^{-i\theta}
\end{align}
where $\theta = \arctan \tan \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \tanh \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}} $. As $\tan x \leq 2x$ for $ 0\leq x \leq 1$ and $\tanh x \leq x$, we have:
\begin{equation}
    |\theta| \leq \tan \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \tanh \left|\frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right| \leq \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta}}\left|\frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right|\leq \frac{\nu\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}
\end{equation}
Hence, for $\eta \geq 3$,
\begin{align}
   \operatorname{Re} \left[\cos^{2b}\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} +\frac{i \pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right) \right] &= \left(\cos^2 \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \cosh^2 \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}}+\sin^2 \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \sinh^2 \frac{ \pi y}{2^{\eta}} \right)^{b} \cos (2b \theta) \\
   &\geq \cos^{2b} \frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \cos (2b\theta)\\
   & \geq \left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{2\eta+3}}\right)^{2b} \cos (2b\theta)\\
   & \geq \left(1-2b\frac{\pi^2}{2^{2\eta+2}}\right)\cos (2b\theta)
\end{align}
If $b \leq 2^{\eta-1}$ then $|2b\theta| \leq \frac{b\nu \pi}{2^{\eta+1}} \leq \frac{\pi}{4}$ and $2b\frac{\pi^2}{2^{2\eta+2}} \leq \frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}$, and hence $\cos (2b \theta) \geq \cos \frac{\pi}{4} > \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ and $ 1-2b\frac{\pi^2}{2^{2\eta+2}}\geq 1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}$. Hence:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Re} \left[\cos^{2b}\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}} +\frac{i \pi y}{2^{\eta}}\right) \right] \geq \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}\right)
\end{equation}
As $|\operatorname{sech} w| \leq \operatorname{csch}(\operatorname{Re} w)$, we have:
\begin{align}
    \left|\alpha(\pm2^{\eta-1}-\frac12+iy+2^{\eta-1})\right| &\leq \operatorname{csch}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}\right)\right)\\
    &\leq 3e^{-\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}\right)}
\end{align}
for $r > \frac{\sqrt{2}}{1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}}$. Therefore, we have:
\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_b \leq \max\left(2r\left(
\left(\frac{\pi}{2^{\eta+1}}\right)^2+\nu^2
\right)^{b},3e^{-\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{\pi^2}{2^{\eta+2}}\right)}\right)
\end{equation}
As the choice of $r$ and $b$ has no impact upon the cost of the algorithm we are free to choose them as large as we like (up to $b\leq 2^{\eta-1}$), and hence we can always suppress $\epsilon_b$ such that the dominant source of error is given by $\epsilon_{\Delta} = O(e^{-\nu\mu})$. However, it should be noted that the larger we choose $b$ the smaller the lower bound on the exponent of this exponential decay.

\section{Proofs of Lemmas used to bound the diamond-norm error of the band-limited maps}
\label{section:appendixTrunc}
\begin{lemma*}[\ref{lemma:0}]
% \label{lemma:0}
    Define $\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}$ as given by:
    \begin{equation}
       \widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}=\sum_j \sum_{p,q} \tilde G^{pq}_j \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q} \otimes \ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}
    \end{equation}
    where 
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde G^{pq}_j = \begin{pmatrix}
        (-1)^{q-p}\tilde\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && \tilde\beta(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)\\
        -\tilde\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt) && (-1)^{q-p}\tilde\alpha(q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)
    \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation}
    Then,  
    \begin{equation}
        \|\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}-\text{BSI-QPE}\| \leq 2\epsilon_{\Delta}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
    As both operators are block diagonal in the Hamiltonian eigenbasis, we will have the operator norm given by the supremum over $\ket{\psi_j}$:
    \begin{equation}
        \|\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}-\text{BSI-QPE}\| = \max_j 
        \left\|\sum_{p,q}(\tilde G^{pq}_j - G^{pq}_j) \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q}\right\|
    \end{equation}
    As $\|U-V\| \leq \sqrt{\|U-V\|_{1}\|U-V\|_{\infty}}$ we can bound the spectral norm by bounding the $l^1$ (maximum absolute column sum) and $l^{\infty}$ (maximum absolute row sum) operator norms. First the $l^1$ operator norm:
    \begin{multline}
         \left\|\sum_{p,q}(\tilde G^{pq}_j - G^{pq}_j) \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q}\right\|_1 = \max_q\sum_{p}|\tilde\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)-\hat\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)|+\\|\tilde\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)-\hat\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)|
    \end{multline}
    which simplifies to:
    \begin{equation}
         \left\|\sum_{p,q}(\tilde G^{pq}_j - G^{pq}_j) \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q}\right\|_1 = \max_q\sum_{\substack{p\\|q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt|_{2^{\eta}}>\mu}}|\hat\alpha(q-p+\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)|+\sum_{\substack{p\\|q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt|_{2^{\eta}}>\mu}}|\hat\beta (q-p-\frac{2^{\eta}}{2\pi}E_jt)|
    \end{equation}
    Therefore, using \eqref{eq:scaling} we can see:
    \begin{equation}
        \left\|\sum_{p,q}(\tilde G^{pq}_j - G^{pq}_j) \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q}\right\|_1 = O(e^{-\nu \mu})
    \end{equation}
    A similar process gives us:
    \begin{equation}
        \left\|\sum_{p,q}(\tilde G^{pq}_j - G^{pq}_j) \otimes \ket{p}\bra{q}\right\|_{\infty} =O(e^{-\nu \mu})
    \end{equation}
    Thus,
    \begin{equation}
        \|\widetilde{\text{BSI-QPE}}-\text{BSI-QPE}\| \leq 2\epsilon_{\Delta} = O\left(e^{-\nu \mu}\right)
    \end{equation}
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma*}[\ref{lemma:3}]
% \label{lemma:3}
Let $\{D_s\}_{s\in S}$ be mutually orthogonal projectors satisfying $\sum_{s\in S} D_s \leq \mathbb{I}$. Given unitaries $U$ and $V$, define the measurement $K_s = U^{\dagger}D_s V$ and CP map $\mathcal{K}[\rho] = \sum_{s \in S} K_s\rho K_s^{\dagger}$. Further, given operators $\tilde U, \tilde V$, define approximate measurement operators $\tilde K_s = \tilde U^{\dagger}D_s \tilde V$ and the approximate map $\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[\rho] = \sum_{s \in S} \tilde K_s\rho \tilde K_s^{\dagger}$. If $\|U-\tilde U\| \leq \epsilon<\frac14$ and $\|V-\tilde V\| \leq \epsilon<\frac14$, then 
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{K} - \tilde{\mathcal{K}}\|_{\diamond} \leq 6\epsilon
\end{equation}
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
First, consider:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{K}[\rho] - \tilde{\mathcal{K}}[\rho] &= \sum_{s \in S} K_s\rho K_s^{\dagger} - \tilde K_s\rho \tilde K_s^{\dagger} \\
    &= \sum_{s \in S} (K_s-\tilde K_s)\rho K_s^{\dagger} +\tilde K_s\rho (K_s^{\dagger}- \tilde K_s^{\dagger}) \label{eq:ksUdiff}
\end{align}
For $\Phi[\rho]=\sum_sR_{1,s}\rho R_{2,s}^\dagger$, define
$V_i=\sum_s|s\rangle\otimes R_{i,s}$. For any reference system \(R\)
and operator $\rho$, the Schatten trace-norm duality gives
\begin{align}
\|(\Phi\otimes\operatorname{id}_R)[\rho]\|_*
&=\sup_{\|Y\|\leq1}
\left|Tr\!\left[
(\mathbb I_S\otimes Y)
(V_1\otimes\mathbb I_R) \rho(V_2^\dagger\otimes\mathbb I_R)
\right]\right|\\
&\leq \|V_1\|\,\|V_2\|\,\|\rho\|_*.
\end{align}
As $\|\Phi\|_{\diamond} = \sup_R\sup_{\|\rho\|_*\leq 1}\|(\Phi\otimes\operatorname{id}_R)[\rho]\|_*$ and
\(V_i^\dagger V_i=\sum_sR_{i,s}^\dagger R_{i,s}\), we have
\begin{equation}
\|\Phi\|_\diamond
\leq
\left\|\sum_{s\in S}R_{1,s}^\dagger R_{1,s}\right\|^{1/2}
\left\|\sum_{s\in S}R_{2,s}^\dagger R_{2,s}\right\|^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
Applying the above bound to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ksUdiff}
gives
\begin{equation}
\|\mathcal{K}-\tilde{\mathcal{K}}\|_\diamond
\leq
\left\|\sum_{s\in S}(K_s^\dagger - \tilde K_s^\dagger)(K_s - \tilde K_s)\right\|^{1/2}
\left(
\left\|\sum_{s\in S}K_s^\dagger K_s\right\|^{1/2}
+
\left\|\sum_{s\in S}\tilde K_s^\dagger\tilde K_s\right\|^{1/2}
\right).
\label{eq:row-column-bound}
\end{equation}
Let us evaluate the terms in this expression. First, we have
\begin{equation}
     \left\|\sum_{s\in S} K_s^{\dagger}  K_s \right\| = \left\|\sum_{s\in S}  V^{\dagger}D_sV \right\| \leq  \left\|V^{\dagger} V\right\| = 1
\end{equation}

Second, we have
\begin{align}
    \left\|\sum_{s\in S}\tilde K_s^{\dagger} \tilde K_s \right\|^{\frac{1}{2}} &= \left\|\sum_{s\in S} \tilde V^{\dagger} D_s \tilde U\tilde U^{\dagger}  D_s \tilde V\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}} \\
     &\leq \|\tilde U\|\left\|\sum_{s\in S} \tilde V^{\dagger} D_s  \tilde V\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
     &\leq \|\tilde U\| \|\tilde V^{\dagger}  \tilde V\|^{\frac{1}{2}}= \|\tilde U\|\|\tilde V\| \leq (1+\epsilon)^2\\
\end{align}
Finally let us consider:
\begin{align}
    K_s-\tilde K_s &= U^{\dagger}  D_s V-\tilde U^{\dagger}  D_s \tilde V\\
    &=  (U-\tilde U)^{\dagger}  D_s V+\tilde U^{\dagger}  D_s (V-\tilde V)\\
\end{align}
Let us define $E_U = U - \tilde U$, then:
\begin{align}
    \left\|\sum_{s\in S}(K_s^\dagger - \tilde K_s^\dagger)(K_s - \tilde K_s)\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}&= \left\| \sum_{s \in S}\ket{s} \otimes ( E_U^{\dagger}  D_s V+\tilde U^{\dagger}  D_sE_V)\right\|\\
    &\leq  \left\| \sum_{s \in S} \ket{s} \otimes E_U^{\dagger}  D_s V \right\| + \left\| \sum_{s \in S} \ket{s} \otimes \tilde U^{\dagger}  D_sE_V\right\|\\
    &\leq \left\| \sum_{s \in S}  V^{\dagger}  D_sE_UE_U^{\dagger} D_s  V\right\|^{\frac{1}{2}} +  \left\| \sum_{s \in S} E_V^{\dagger}  D_s \tilde U \tilde U^{\dagger} D_s E_V \right\|^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
    &\leq \|E_U\| + \|E_V\|\|\tilde U\| \leq \epsilon(2+\epsilon)
\end{align}

Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{K} - \tilde{\mathcal{K}}\|_{\diamond} \leq \epsilon(2+\epsilon)(1+(1+\epsilon)^2)
\end{equation}
Using $\epsilon<\frac14$, we obtain $\|\mathcal{K} - \tilde {\mathcal{K}}\|_{\diamond} \leq 6\epsilon$.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma*}[\ref{lemma:composition}]
Let $\mathcal{M}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{M}_k$ be completely positive trace-non-increasing maps and let $\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1, \ldots, \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k$ be completely positive maps with $\|\mathcal{M}_i - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq \varepsilon$ for all $i$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1\|_{\diamond} \leq (1+\varepsilon)^k - 1 \leq e k \varepsilon,
\end{equation}
the last inequality holding whenever $k\varepsilon \leq 1$. More generally, if instead $\|\mathcal{M}_i\|_{\diamond}, \|\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq c$ for some $c \geq 1$ and $\|\mathcal{M}_i - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq \varepsilon_i$, without assuming the $\mathcal{M}_i$ are trace-non-increasing, then
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1\|_{\diamond} \leq c^{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_i .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
    Writing the difference as a telescoping sum,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_{i+1} \circ (\mathcal{M}_i - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i) \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{i-1} \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1,
\end{equation}
and using submultiplicativity of the diamond norm together with $\|\mathcal{M}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq 1$ (completely positive trace-non-increasing) and $\|\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_i\|_{\diamond} \leq \|\mathcal{M}_i\|_{\diamond} + \varepsilon \leq 1 + \varepsilon$, we obtain
\begin{equation}
    \|\mathcal{M}_k \circ \cdots \circ \mathcal{M}_1 - \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_k \circ \cdots \circ \tilde{\mathcal{M}}_1\|_{\diamond} \leq \sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon(1+\varepsilon)^{i-1} = (1+\varepsilon)^k - 1.
\end{equation}
For $k\varepsilon \leq 1$ we have $(1+\varepsilon)^k \leq e^{k\varepsilon} \leq 1 + e k\varepsilon$, giving the final inequality. For the general statement, the $i$-th term of the same telescoping sum has diamond norm at most $c^{k-i}\,\varepsilon_i\,c^{i-1} = c^{k-1}\varepsilon_i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma*}[\ref{lemma:summed-history-reversal}]
For $n\geq1$, let $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{n+1}$ and $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n+1}$ denote
the compound labels
\begin{equation*}
    y_i=(b_{2i-1},d_i),\qquad x_i=(c_{2i-1},d'_i),
\end{equation*}
with $a,e$ fixed.  Choose $\theta_x\in[0,\pi/2]$ so that
\begin{equation*}
    W_x=W_{bd}=\begin{pmatrix}
                \sqrt{1 - f_{abde}} & \sqrt{f_{abde}}\\
       \sqrt{f_{abde}} & -\sqrt{1 - f_{abde}}
    \end{pmatrix}= \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_x&\sin\theta_x\\
    \sin\theta_x&-\cos\theta_x\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation*}
and set $\Delta_i=\theta_{y_i}-\theta_{x_i}$.  Then
\begin{equation}
    \bar g^{ae}(\{b\},\{c\},\{d\},\{d'\})
    =\frac12\cos\Delta_1\cos\Delta_{n+1}
      \prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\Delta_i-\Delta_{i+1}).
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    \bar g^{ae}(\{b\},\{c\},\{d\},\{d'\})
    =\bar g^{ae}(\{c\}^R,\{b\}^R,\{d'\}^R,\{d\}^R).
    \label{eq:PRR}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
Put $\ket{v_s(x)}=W_x\ket{s}$, so
$R_s^x=\ket{v_s(x)}\bra{v_s(x)}$. Therefore, we can rewrite $\bar g^{ae}$ as:
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s_0,\ldots,s_n} \braket{0|v_{s_0}(x_1)}\braket{v_{s_0}(x_1)|v_{s_1}(x_2)}\ldots \braket{v_{s_{n-1}}(x_n)|v_{s_n}(x_{n+1})}\braket{v_{s_n}(x_{n+1})|v_{s_n}(y_{n+1})}\\\braket{v_{s_n}(y_{n+1})|v_{s_{n-1}}(y_n)}\ldots\braket{v_{s_1}(y_2)|v_{s_0}(y_1)}\braket{v_{s_0}(y_1)|0}
\end{multline}
We can reorder this to get:
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s_0,\ldots,s_n} \braket{0|v_{s_0}(x_1)}\braket{v_{s_0}(y_1)|0}\braket{v_{s_0}(x_1)|v_{s_1}(x_2)}\braket{v_{s_1}(y_2)|v_{s_0}(y_1)}\ldots\\ \braket{v_{s_{n-1}}(x_{n})|v_{s_{n}}(x_{n+1})}\braket{v_{s_{n}}(y_{n+1})|v_{s_{n-1}}(y_{n})}\braket{v_{s_{n}}(x_{n+1})|v_{s_{n}}(y_{n+1})}
\end{multline}
For consecutive labels define
the two-state transfer matrix
\begin{equation*}
    (T_i)_{uv}=\braket{v_u(y_i)|v_v(y_{i+1})}
    \braket{v_v(x_{i+1})|v_u(x_i)}.
\end{equation*}
Therefore, we can rewrite $\bar g$ as:
\begin{equation}
   \frac{1}{2}\sum_{s_0,\ldots,s_n} h_{s_0}(T_1)_{s_0s_1}(T_2)_{s_1s_2}\ldots (T_n)_{s_{n-1}s_{n}} c_{s_n}
\end{equation}
where $h_s = \braket{0|v_{s}(x_1)}\braket{v_{s}(y_1)|0}$ and $c_s =\braket{v_{s}(x_{n+1})|v_{s}(y_{n+1})}$. Hence, we can write $\bar g$ as simple matrix multiplication:
\begin{equation}
    \bar g(\{b\},\{c\},\{d\},\{d'\}) = \frac{1}{2}hT_1T_2T_3\ldots T_nc
\end{equation}
As $W_xW_y = \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos (\theta_x - \theta_y) & - \sin (\theta_x - \theta_y)\\
     \sin (\theta_x - \theta_y) & \cos (\theta_x - \theta_y)
\end{pmatrix}$ we have:
\begin{align}
    (T_i)_{u v} &=  \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}}) & - \sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\\
     \sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}}) & \cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})
\end{pmatrix}_{uv} \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) & - \sin (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}})\\
     \sin (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) & \cos (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) 
\end{pmatrix}_{vu}\\
&=\begin{pmatrix}
    \cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\cos (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) & - \sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\sin (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}})\\
     -\sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\sin (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) & \cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}}) \cos (\theta_{x_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i}}) 
\end{pmatrix}_{uv}\\
&=  \cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\cos (\theta_{x_{i}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}}) \mathbb{I}_{uv} + \sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\sin (\theta_{x_{i}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}}) X_{uv}
\end{align}
We can also clearly see that $c = \cos (\theta_{x_{n+1}} -\theta_{y_{n+1}}) \begin{pmatrix}
    1\\1
\end{pmatrix} =  \cos(\Delta_{n+1})\begin{pmatrix}
    1\\1
\end{pmatrix} $, and we have that $\begin{pmatrix}
    1\\1
\end{pmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of all $T_i$ as $T_i = \alpha_i \mathbb{I} + \beta_i X$. The corresponding eigenvalue for each $T_i$ is given by:
\begin{align}
    \alpha_i+\beta_i&=\cos (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\cos (\theta_{x_{i}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}}) + \sin (\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}})\sin (\theta_{x_{i}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}}) \\
    &= \cos(\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{y_{i+1}} - (\theta_{x_{i}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}}))\\
    &= \cos\!\left(\theta_{y_i} - \theta_{x_i} - (\theta_{y_{i+1}} - \theta_{x_{i+1}})\right)\\
    &= \cos(\Delta_i - \Delta_{i+1})
\end{align}
Therefore, $T_1T_2T_3\ldots T_nc = \cos(\Delta_{n+1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\Delta_i - \Delta_{i+1})\begin{pmatrix}
    1\\1
\end{pmatrix}$. As $h = \begin{pmatrix}
    \cos(\theta_{x_1})\cos(\theta_{y_1})& \sin(\theta_{x_1})\sin(\theta_{y_1})
\end{pmatrix}$, we have $h\begin{pmatrix}
    1\\1
\end{pmatrix}= \cos(\theta_{x_1})\cos(\theta_{y_1}) + \sin(\theta_{x_1})\sin(\theta_{y_1}) = \cos(\theta_{x_1}-\theta_{y_1}) = \cos(\Delta_1)$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
    \bar g(\{b\},\{c\},\{d\},\{d'\}) =\frac{1}{2}\cos(\Delta_1)\cos(\Delta_{n+1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\Delta_i - \Delta_{i+1})
\end{equation}
  Exchanging $b,d$ ($x$) with $c,d'$ ($y$) maps $\Delta_i\mapsto-\Delta_i$, and $\bar g$ is preserved because cosine is even. Reversing $\{b\}$, $\{c\}$, $\{d\}$ and $\{d'\}$ maps $\Delta_i\mapsto\Delta_{n+2-i}$. As $\frac{1}{2}\cos(\Delta_{n+1})\cos(\Delta_{1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\Delta_{n+2-i} - \Delta_{n+1-i}) = \frac{1}{2}\cos(\Delta_1)\cos(\Delta_{n+1})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\cos(\Delta_i - \Delta_{i+1})$, the quantity $\bar g$ is preserved and Eq.~\eqref{eq:PRR} holds.
\end{proof}
\begin{lemma*}[\ref{lemma:wdrift} $W$-drift]
% \label{lemma:wdrift}
For all $a,b,d,e$ and all $|z|_{2^{\eta}} \leq (4n+6)\mu$,
\begin{equation}
    \|W^F_{a-z,b-z,de} - W^F_{abde}\| \leq 2\sqrt{\beta\frac{6\pi}{2^{\eta}t}}\sqrt{(4n+6)\mu}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma*}
\begin{proof}
    By definition we have
\begin{equation}
     W^F_{a-z,b-z,de} = \begin{pmatrix}
        \sqrt{1 - F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})} & \sqrt{F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})}\\
       \sqrt{F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})} & -\sqrt{1 - F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})}
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{equation}
    F(\kappa) = \begin{cases}1& \text{ for }  -2^{\eta-1} \leq \kappa <  -3\cdot 2^{\eta-3}\\
        \min(1,e^{-\beta \frac{2\pi}{2^{\eta}t} \kappa}) & \text{ for } |\kappa|\leq 3\cdot 2^{\eta-3}\\
        e^{-\beta\frac{3\pi}{4t}} + \frac{1- e^{-\beta\frac{3\pi}{4t}}}{2^{\eta-3}}(\kappa - 3\cdot 2^{\eta-3}) & \text{ for }  3\cdot 2^{\eta-3} < \kappa <  2^{\eta-1}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \kappa_{a-z,b-z,de} \equiv (-1)^d(b-z) - (-1)^e(a-z) \mod 2^{\eta} \text{ for } -2^{\eta-1} \leq \kappa_{a-z,b-z,de} < 2^{\eta-1}
\end{equation}
As $|z|_{2^{\eta}} \leq (4n+6)\mu< 2^{\eta-3}$  we have:
\begin{equation}
    |\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de} - \kappa_{abde}|_{2^{\eta}} \leq 2(4n+6)\mu
\end{equation}
The function $F$ is continuous and $2^{\eta}$-periodic, so its Lipschitz constant with respect to the circular distance $|\cdot|_{2^{\eta}}$ is given by its largest gradient. For $|\kappa|\leq 3\cdot 2^{\eta-3}$ the largest gradient is given by $\frac{2\pi\beta}{2^{\eta}t}$, whereas for $3\cdot 2^{\eta-3} < \kappa <  2^{\eta-1}$ the gradient is given by $\frac{1- e^{-\beta\frac{3\pi}{4t}}}{2^{\eta-3}} \leq \frac{6\pi\beta}{2^{\eta}t}$. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
     |F(\kappa)-F(\kappa')|\leq \frac{6\pi\beta}{2^{\eta}t}|\kappa - \kappa'|_{2^{\eta}} \qquad \forall \quad \kappa, \kappa'
\end{equation}
Hence,
\begin{equation}
    |F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})-F(\kappa_{abde})|\leq 12(4n+6)\frac{\pi\mu\beta}{2^{\eta}t}
\end{equation}
We have
\begin{equation}
    W^F_{a-z,b-z,de}-W^F_{abde}=\begin{pmatrix} p & q\\ q & -p\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $p=\sqrt{1-F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})}-\sqrt{1-F(\kappa_{abde})}$ and $q=\sqrt{F(\kappa_{a-z,b-z,de})}-\sqrt{F(\kappa_{abde})}$. This matrix is real symmetric and squares to $(p^2+q^2)\mathbb{I}$, so its operator norm is $\sqrt{p^2+q^2}$. Since $|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$ for all $x,y\ge 0$, we have $|p|\leq \sqrt{12(4n+6)\frac{\pi\mu\beta}{2^{\eta}t}}$ and $|q|\leq \sqrt{12(4n+6)\frac{\pi\mu\beta}{2^{\eta}t}}$. Therefore
\begin{equation}
    \|W^F_{a-z,b-z,de}-W^F_{abde}\|\le\sqrt{24(4n+6)\frac{\pi\mu\beta}{2^{\eta}t}}
    =2\sqrt{\beta\frac{6\pi}{2^{\eta}t}}\sqrt{(4n+6)\mu}
\end{equation}
\end{proof}
